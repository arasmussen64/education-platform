# Topic 47: `csa.sec.zero-trust`, Zero trust

Designer's brief, 2026-11-12 (designer key `threat-modeling-zero-trust`; with [csa.sec.threat-modeling.md](csa.sec.threat-modeling.md)). Prototypes are in `prototypes/tm-zt/`: `ztlab2/` is the build, on the network-lab base, with oauth2-proxy and nginx as the access proxy, a stand-in identity provider (`mini-idp`, for the prototype only: the real labs use the identity-lab base), per-machine nftables, check-made users and devices, its contract kept with the restart trial (`contract-zt-v3.log`; `contract-zt-v2.log` is the run before the new-machine probe); `ztlab/` and `contract-zt-run2.log` are the first version, whose two passing wrong fixes and failed restart trial taught the fixes listed in section 9; `ztdebug/` holds the debug's faults and fix. `tools/contract.py` is a stand-in for `labcheck test` (the repository is read-only here).

## 1. What the learner will be able to do

- State zero trust as NIST SP 800-207 does (no implicit trust from network location; least privilege per request; a network viewed as compromised), name its seven tenets in their own words, and point at the policy engine, policy administrator and policy enforcement point in a real system.
- Put an **identity-aware access proxy** in front of internal apps so each request is decided by who is asking, from which device, for which app, and make the apps reachable only through it: the resource-portal model, built with nginx and oauth2-proxy.
- Treat the **device** as part of the identity: a device certificate from a device CA, checked by the proxy (mutual TLS from `csa.net.certificates`), and posture signals from device management; know why a header the client sends proves nothing.
- **Micro-segment** a network: per-service allow rules on each machine, inbound and outbound, proven by traffic from every side, including a new machine an attacker might add; and say how security groups, application security groups and service-account firewall rules do it on the three clouds.
- Design **continuous verification**: session refresh and revocation, and work out how long a revoked user or device keeps access (the exposure window), on the lab's clock and the clouds' (Entra's continuous access evaluation, token lifetimes).
- Explain how **zero trust network access** replaces a remote-access VPN, what it costs to move, and what can go wrong (the proxy as a single point of failure, apps reachable around it, non-HTTP apps).

**The six-month core.** Being on the network proves nothing; every request is decided at an enforcement point by identity (user and device) and policy, as close to the resource as possible, and the resource answers nobody else. A VPN gives a device the network; an access proxy gives a person one app. Device trust is a credential the enterprise issued (a certificate), never something the client says. Segmentation is proved by traffic, from every machine, including a new one. Access must end when trust does, so know your exposure window: refresh interval plus whatever caches sit after it.

## 4. Lesson plan

The lesson supplies what no resource does for a beginner: the VPN-to-proxy picture drawn on the lab's own machines, the NIST vocabulary laid over nginx and oauth2-proxy line by line, and the exposure-window arithmetic.

1. **Why "inside" stopped meaning "safe"** (Z2, the BeyondCorp article, "The Major Components of BeyondCorp" and "Removing Trust from the Network", 12 min). *Picture in words:* the IPsec topic's office with its VPN, redrawn for remote staff: a laptop at home dials in and receives an address on the office network; from then on the firewall treats it as the office, so it reaches the wiki, payroll, the database and every SSH port, and so does any malware on it. One stolen password plus a VPN client is the whole estate (NIST SP 800-207 §2: "unauthorized lateral movement within the environment has been one of the biggest challenges").
2. **NIST's definition and the seven tenets** (Z1, NIST SP 800-207 §2 and §2.1, 15 min). *What the lesson adds:* each tenet in one plain sentence beside what it means in the lab: "all communication is secured regardless of network location" → TLS to the proxy from inside and out; "access ... granted on a per-session basis" → a decision per request at the proxy; "dynamic policy" → groups, device and time; "monitors and measures the integrity and security posture of all owned and associated assets" → device certificates and posture; "dynamic and strictly enforced before access is allowed" → nothing reaches an app except through the PEP; "collects as much information as possible" → the proxy's logs. The airport picture (§2): the checkpoint is the PDP/PEP and the gate area the implicit trust zone; zero trust shrinks the zone to one app.
3. **The parts: PE, PA, PEP** (Z1 §3, 8 min). *The lab mapped onto Figure 2:* nginx on `zt` is the PEP (it lets a request through or not); oauth2-proxy's `/oauth2/auth` plus the identity provider make the decision (PE and PA together, as NIST says many products combine them); the device CA is the enterprise PKI; the identity provider is the ID management system; the proxy's logs feed the SIEM. The trust algorithm in one paragraph: criteria-based (all of these must hold: in hr, on a managed device) against score-based; singular against contextual (§3.3.1's HR clerk opening 100 records in a day).
4. **Approaches and deployment models** (Z1 §3.1 and §3.2, 8 min). Identity governance, micro-segmentation and the software-defined perimeter; "a full ZT solution will include elements of all three". The four deployment models, and which the labs build: the **resource portal** (one proxy in front of several apps, no agent on the laptop; NIST's caveat: the portal sees the device only when it connects, and attackers can find and attack the portal), plus **micro-segmentation** behind it.
5. **The access proxy, line by line** (Z3, oauth2-proxy's nginx integration page, 8 min). `auth_request` sends each request's headers to oauth2-proxy first; 202 lets it through, 401 means "not signed in" (nginx sends the browser to `/oauth2/start`, which redirects to the identity provider: topic 44's authorization code flow), 403 means "signed in, not allowed" (`?allowed_groups=hr`). The proxy passes the user's name to the app in a header the app trusts only because nothing else can reach it, which is why step 7 matters. *Two traps the lesson names, both found in the prototype:* oauth2-proxy reads `client_secret_file` byte for byte, so a trailing newline gets `invalid_client` from the identity provider; and with `cookie_refresh`, the refreshed session comes back as a `Set-Cookie` on the decision's answer, which nginx drops unless the config passes it on (`auth_request_set $auth_cookie $upstream_http_set_cookie; add_header Set-Cookie $auth_cookie always;`), so sessions die at their first refresh with a provider that rotates refresh tokens.
6. **The device is part of who you are** (Z2, "Securely Identifying the Device", 5 min; `csa.net.certificates` recalled). BeyondCorp's rule: a device gets a certificate only while it is "present and correct in the Device Inventory Database", renewed periodically, and the certificate "does not single-handedly grant access privileges". In the lab: a device CA kept for clients alone; nginx asks every client for a certificate (`ssl_verify_client optional`, so the wiki stays open to any device) and the payroll location refuses anything but `$ssl_client_verify = SUCCESS`. *The trap, on purpose:* `optional_no_ca` with a test for `NONE` lets in a certificate from any CA, so a laptop with a self-made certificate passes. Posture beyond the certificate: patch level, disk encryption and endpoint protection from device management (MDM; topic 41's compliance); a header like `X-Device-Compliant: yes` from the client is worthless (NIST §3.4.1: "not using information that cannot be authenticated (e.g., network MAC addresses that can be spoofed)").
7. **Micro-segmentation: nothing reaches an app except through the proxy** (Z1 §3.1.2 and §3.4.1 item 4, 4 min; Module 1's firewall recalled). Each app machine's own nftables: input `policy drop`, accept its port only from the proxy's address; output `policy drop` too, with the one database connection it needs; the database accepts only its app. *Picture in words:* the apps network as rooms with locked doors and one corridor (the proxy). Prove it the way Module 1 did, by traffic from every machine, and add a new machine to the network to see what an attacker who lands one gets: in the first prototype a wiki rule that accepted the whole subnet passed every check, because every existing machine's own egress rules hid it; only a new machine showed it. *Where the clouds differ* (S1 to S3): AWS security groups allow only (no deny rules) and can name another security group as the source, so "the web servers' group may reach the database on 5432" survives address changes; Azure NSGs have deny rules and priorities and a default `AllowVNetInBound` that lets everything in the virtual network talk, so segmentation needs explicit deny rules, with application security groups naming the tiers; Google's firewall rules should target service accounts, because "a network tag is an arbitrary attribute" that anyone who can edit the VM can change.
8. **Continuous verification and the exposure window** (Z4, Entra's continuous access evaluation, the "Overview", "Critical event evaluation" and "Token lifetime" parts, 8 min). Without anything continuous, a signed-in session lasts as long as its cookie (oauth2-proxy's default: 168 hours). With `cookie_refresh`, the proxy asks the identity provider again after that long, and a disabled user's refresh fails ("Fatal refresh error detected (session revoked or invalid), clearing session", captured); the window is at most the refresh interval plus one request. On Entra: access tokens last an hour by default; with CAE, critical events (account disabled, password reset, refresh tokens revoked, high user risk) reach CAE-aware apps "near real time, but latency of up to 15 minutes might be observed", CAE tokens last up to 28 hours, and group membership and policy changes can take "up to one day". *The arithmetic the lesson supplies:* worst-case window = time until the next re-check + propagation. And the TLS trap: a resumed TLS session (nginx's `ssl_session_timeout` defaults to 5 minutes, session tickets on) reuses the original handshake, so a device check made at the handshake is only as fresh as the session.
9. **ZTNA against the VPN, and the move** (Z1 §3.4.1 items 6 to 8 and §5, 6 min; Z5, Entra Private Access concept page, 4 min; Z6, Google IAP overview, 5 min; Z7, AWS Verified Access, 5 min). NIST §3.4.1 item 8: remote staff "should not be required to use a link back to the enterprise network (i.e., virtual private network [VPN])". *The comparison the lesson draws, on the lab's machines:* VPN = an address on the network, every port of every machine; ZTNA = one app at a time, decided per request, the apps never exposed. *Where the clouds differ:* AWS Verified Access evaluates each request against Cedar policies with an identity trust provider and device trust providers (CrowdStrike, Jamf, JumpCloud), denies everything until a policy exists, and is charged per application hour and per GB; Entra Private Access (part of Global Secure Access, Microsoft's SSE) uses a client on the laptop and private network connectors, with Quick Access for a range and per-app access for TCP and UDP apps under Conditional Access, licensed through the Microsoft Entra Suite or standalone on top of Entra ID P1 or P2; Google's IAP checks sign-in and the "IAP-secured Web App User" role per request, can add context-aware access, and warns that anyone who can reach a VM's serving port directly bypasses it (firewall rules and Cloud Run's ingress settings close that). The move, in steps: inventory apps and who uses them; put the first app behind the proxy beside the VPN; close the app's direct path; repeat; take the VPN's route into the apps network away; keep a break-glass path (topic 41). The risks NIST lists (§5.2): the PEP and PA become the thing to knock over, "also possible in legacy remote-access VPNs".
10. **Maturity, briefly** (Z8, CISA's Zero Trust Maturity Model v2.0, the pillars page, 4 min). Five pillars (Identity, Devices, Networks, Applications and Workloads, Data), three cross-cutting capabilities (Visibility and Analytics, Automation and Orchestration, Governance), four stages (Traditional, Initial, Advanced, Optimal). The CISA page is now marked archived and points to the federal zero-trust hub; the model is still the common vocabulary. The UK NCSC's principles (Z9) say the same in seven or eight lines.

**Read now, in this order:** Z1's §2 to §3.2 and §3.4.1 (about 35 minutes; the main resource), Z2 (25), Z3 (8), Z4's three parts (8). Z5 to Z9 are "more resources".

### Build: `build-replace-the-vpn` (systemd, identity base, 60 minutes; prototyped, contract kept)

**The brief.** The shared network above, the apps reachable today only over the VPN, `zt` a fresh server with nginx and oauth2-proxy installed and nothing configured (its client secret, made at build time, is in `/etc/oauth2-proxy/client-secret`; the device CA's certificate in `/etc/nginx/tls/`). When you're done: (1) staff reach the wiki at `https://apps.corp.test/wiki/` after signing in, from any device; (2) payroll at `/payroll/` answers only people in hr, only on a managed device (a certificate from the device CA); (3) nothing reaches the wiki, payroll or the database except through `zt`, and the database answers only payroll; a new machine on the apps network reaches none of them; (4) the VPN no longer reaches the apps network; (5) someone disabled at the identity provider loses access within 15 seconds (the lab's fast clock; in production a few minutes); (6) all of it survives a reboot. ### Debug: `debug-who-gets-in` (systemd, identity base, 40 minutes; faults prototyped)

**The story.** The proxy went live last month. The incident report: a contractor's account pulled salary data through payroll's phone-app API; an unmanaged laptop with a certificate its owner made reached payroll; and the wiki server, compromised last week through an old plugin, made connections to payroll's database. **Three faults.** Rules: payroll's API keeps working for hr on managed devices; the wiki stays open to staff on any device; don't turn device checks into a client-sent header; nothing opened wider than a job needs.

**The faults** (prototype `ztdebug/faults.sh` against `fixed.sh`): a `location /payroll/api/` added in October with `proxy_pass` and no `auth_request` (the more specific location wins in nginx, so the API skips payroll's policy); `ssl_verify_client optional_no_ca` with `if ($ssl_client_verify = NONE)`; the database's nftables accepting 5432 from `10.20.0.0/24`. Each maps to one check: `payroll-api-follows-payroll` (new: `ztdebug/api-check.sh`), `payroll-hr-on-managed-devices`, `no-lateral-moves`. The other build checks run as "what the repair must not break". Wrong fixes: `api-forbidden` (`return 403` for the whole API: fails the hr user's API call), `verify-on-everywhere` (`ssl_verify_client on` for the server: fails the wiki from `byod`), `db-closed` (nothing accepted: payroll loses its database), `live-only` (`nft add rule` on db), `api-staff-group` (the API location asks for staff).

**Run, not yet as a full contract** (`tools/trial.py`, 2026-11-12): with the three faults applied, exactly three checks fail, each the one aimed at its fault (`payroll-hr-on-managed-devices`: "payroll answered a device whose certificate isn't from the device CA"; `no-lateral-moves`: "from zt, 10.20.0.12 port 5432 is open"; the API check), and the other four pass; with the fix, all seven pass. The wrong fixes are designed, not run; expected contract time about 6 minutes. These prototype messages say more than a debug's may (the second names the machine and port); the real lab's messages state only the end state still missing ("the database answers a machine other than payroll"), never where. The debug also adds one assertion the build doesn't need: staff on `byod`, with no device certificate, still get the wiki (it is what catches `verify-on-everywhere`).

### Explain (20 minutes)

**Prompt.** "Your company's staff reach internal apps over a VPN. The CISO wants 'zero trust' by next year. Explain, in your own words: what NIST means by zero trust, and why being on the company network should prove nothing; the parts that decide and enforce each request, pointing at what plays each part in a design you'd build; how the device becomes part of the decision and why a header the client sends doesn't count; how you'd make sure nothing reaches an app except through the enforcement point, and how you'd prove it; how access ends when an employee leaves, and how long they might keep it in your design; and what moving from the VPN to zero trust network access involves, including one thing that gets worse. Say how the three clouds' services for this differ."

**Rubric** (required unless marked): `definition` (per-request, least-privilege decisions; no implicit trust from network location; the network viewed as compromised); `components` (PE and PA decide, the PEP enforces; mapped to a proxy and an identity provider; logs and device data as inputs); `device` (an issued credential such as a device certificate, renewed while healthy; posture from device management; client-supplied claims and MAC addresses prove nothing); `segmentation` (each app answers only the PEP; default deny both ways; proved by traffic, including from a new machine); `continuous` (sessions re-checked against the identity provider; disabling a user ends access within the refresh interval; a stated window); `migration-and-risks` (app by app beside the VPN, close direct paths, remove the VPN's routes; the PEP as a single point of failure or DoS target; non-HTTP apps; break-glass); `clouds-differ` (optional: Verified Access's per-request Cedar policies and device trust providers; Entra Private Access's client and connectors under Conditional Access, licensed per user; IAP's IAM role and the bypass warning).

## 13. Open questions (each with a recommendation under the owner's rule)

1. **oauth2-proxy with nginx, or Pomerium, or Authelia.** Pomerium (Apache 2.0, v0.34.1 released 2026-11-11; not tried here, so whether it runs fully offline is unconfirmed) has one policy file per route, closer to NIST's per-request policy, but embeds Envoy and is another new tool; Authelia (Apache 2.0, v4.39.28 of 2026-09-17; GitHub API) has excellent per-request rules but is an identity provider itself, a second one beside the identity-lab base. Recommendation: oauth2-proxy (MIT, the most used open-source access proxy, 15k stars) behind nginx, which the learner configured in four Module 3 topics; best learning experience (one new tool, the rest retrieval), free.
2. **Check-time identities.** Checks add, disable and delete users at the identity provider. Recommendation: allowed, of the same standing as Module 3's throwaway listeners and check-time machines (they change nothing the learner made and are removed); one sentence in ADR-0018's third amendment, Proposed, for the owner queue's "Decided for you".
3. **The VPN as a modelled router.** The lab's `vpngw` forwards a "connected" laptop into the apps network without a tunnel. Recommendation: keep it, say so in the prompt, and cite `csa.net.ipsec`; an IPsec remote-access tunnel adds two daemons and minutes of CI for nothing the checks test.
4. **Path-based or host-based apps.** The prototype serves `apps.corp.test/wiki/` and `/payroll/`; BeyondCorp gives each app its own name. Recommendation: paths, because they make the debug's "a more specific location skips the policy" fault possible and keep one certificate and one cookie domain; the lesson says real deployments usually give each app its own name, and why (cookies scoped per app).
5. **Over the CI aim.** Section 12's measures bring it to about 20 minutes only if the identity provider boots fast. Recommendation: accept up to 25 minutes for this topic if the base's provider can't (identity labs are the module's heaviest, as load balancing and IPsec were Module 3's), and say so in the module brief.
6. **The prerequisite change.** The outline says `csa.m3.checkpoint`; the labs need OIDC (topic 44) and device compliance (topic 41). Recommendation: `prerequisites: [csa.sec.federation, csa.sec.strong-auth]`, a spec-free change (the outline generator leaves a written topic alone).
