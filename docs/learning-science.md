# Learning science

The methods Switchback is built on, and the features they drive. Rule of thumb: if a feature doesn't trace back to one of these, question it.

## Principles → features

| Principle                        | What the research says                                                                                                                                                                                  | Where it shows up                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Retrieval practice**           | Pulling knowledge out of memory strengthens it far more than re-reading it                                                                                                                              | Quizzes, recall prompts, reviews; very little passive re-reading. Questions inside each lesson, right after the paragraph they test (Szpunar et al., 2013). Typed answers, which make the learner produce the answer rather than pick it (Kang et al., 2007; [ADR-0027](decisions/0027-typed-answers.md)) |
| **Spacing**                      | Practice spread out over time beats cramming for long-term retention                                                                                                                                    | The review scheduler (FSRS), with partial credit for topics a later topic uses, a forgetting rate fitted to the learner, a slow right answer counted as Hard, and review labs for hands-on skills ([ADR-0009](decisions/0009-reviews.md)'s amendments of 2026-11-16)                                      |
| **Successive relearning**        | Retrieve until correct, then repeat across spaced sessions; this produces durable knowledge                                                                                                             | "Mastered" only after a delayed review. A missed question comes back as a fresh one on the same skill, after 1, 3 and 7 days, until it's right on 3 different days ([ADR-0011](decisions/0011-mistakes-come-back.md))                                                                                     |
| **Interleaving**                 | Mixing problem types teaches you to _choose_ the right method, not just apply the one you just saw                                                                                                      | Practice and reviews mix current and older topics. "Which one is this?" questions set look-alike ideas side by side, so the learner practises telling them apart (Birnbaum et al., 2013)                                                                                                                  |
| **Mastery learning**             | Move on only when the prerequisites are solid; after a check, corrective practice on what was missed, then a fresh, parallel check                                                                      | Gating with test-out. A missed checkpoint test brings its skill back, and a stuck learner proves the checkpoint on a fresh version ([ADR-0012](decisions/0012-checkpoint-support.md))                                                                                                                     |
| **Worked examples, then fading** | Novices learn more from studying solutions than from solving problems; help should fade as skill grows                                                                                                  | The practice ladder: worked example → half-finished code → blank file. A checkpoint's way through: a solved sibling → the build in steps → a fresh version from a blank editor                                                                                                                            |
| **Whole-task learning (4C/ID)**  | Complex skills transfer best when you practice whole, realistic tasks, simple to complex, with fading support                                                                                           | Projects and "prove it" builds, not just isolated drills                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Self-explanation**             | Explaining _why_ in your own words deepens understanding and exposes gaps                                                                                                                               | Explain-it-back checks                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Desirable difficulties**       | Some struggle (recall, spacing, variation) slows practice but improves long-term learning                                                                                                               | Blank-editor challenges; no answer-giving AI                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Feedback on confident errors** | Mistakes made with high confidence, once corrected, are remembered especially well                                                                                                                      | Immediate, specific feedback. Every quick question asks how sure the learner is as they submit it, and the Account page shows how often each level of certainty is right ([ADR-0028](decisions/0028-confidence-before-the-answer.md))                                                                     |
| **Dual coding / multimedia**     | Words plus well-designed visuals beat words alone                                                                                                                                                       | Diagrams, execution visualizations, embedded videos                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Deliberate practice**          | Expertise tracks hours of focused, effortful practice on tasks just beyond current skill, with feedback (Ericsson et al., 1993); the "10,000 hours" figure is a popularization of that work, not a rule | The practice-hours tracker on the Today page counts only time spent on graded attempts, Checks, explanations and reviews, never time with the site open, and marks milestones from 1 hour to 10,000                                                                                                       |

| **Curiosity and pretesting** | Curiosity before learning improves memory for the answer (Gruber et al., 2014), and guessing before being taught helps even when the guess is wrong (Richland et al., 2009) | Each lesson opens with a puzzle: the learner types a guess, then sees the answer ([ADR-0026](decisions/0026-lesson-building-blocks.md)) |
| **Short sessions, rest and sleep** | Material is learned better in focused blocks; a few minutes of quiet rest after learning helps it stick (Dewar et al., 2012), and sleep consolidates what was learned that day (Diekelmann & Born, 2010) | A topic is shown in parts of about 30 minutes, with a suggested 5-minute break between them ([ADR-0025](decisions/0025-topics-in-parts.md)). Late in the learner's evening, the Today page suggests reviews tonight and new topics tomorrow |
| **Expertise reversal** | Guidance that helps a novice can slow an experienced learner down (Kalyuga et al., 2003) | "Check what you know" makes practice optional for skills already shown, and "Prove it now" skips straight to the proofs. The proofs stay required |
| **Concrete cases** | A real case gives an abstract rule something to hang on | Real incidents from public postmortems, in our own words, each linked to its source. A glossary defines each term where it is used |
| **Low stress during proofs** | Acute stress impairs recall (Shields et al., 2017) | No countdown timers on proofs; checkpoint time targets are stated, never enforced |

## Habits

Learning that takes years depends on coming back. These are the behavioural-science methods the Today page uses. None of them changes what counts as learned.

| Method                                           | What the research says                                                                                                 | Where it shows up                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fewer decisions to start**                     | Every choice before starting is a chance not to start                                                                  | One "Start today's session" button: the most-faded reviews, then mistakes coming back, then the next topic. "Just 5 minutes" for a low day          |
| **Implementation intentions**                    | Deciding when and where you'll act roughly doubles follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006)                         | "When and where will you study?", with a calendar file made in the browser                                                                          |
| **Habits form slowly, and survive a missed day** | Habits take about two months to form, and missing one day doesn't break them (Lally et al., 2010)                      | Practice counted in weeks ("4 of the last 5 weeks"), never as a daily streak; a calm welcome-back plan after a break, spread within the daily limit |
| **Goal gradient and fresh starts**               | People push harder as a goal gets close (Kivetz et al., 2006) and begin more readily at a new start (Dai et al., 2014) | Progress through the current module ("6 of 8 topics proven"), and a new module marked as a fresh start. No points or badges                         |
| **Honest plans**                                 | People underestimate how long tasks take                                                                               | Time figures stretched to the learner's own pace once three topics are proven                                                                       |

## Programming-specific methods

| Method                     | What it is                                                                                                                                       | Where it shows up                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Tracing before writing** | Novices who can't trace code by hand rarely write it well; reading skill comes first                                                             | "Predict the output" exercises early in each topic                                                                       |
| **Parsons problems**       | Rearrange scrambled lines into a working program; teaches structure with less mental load                                                        | Early practice steps                                                                                                     |
| **PRIMM**                  | Predict → Run → Investigate → Modify → Make                                                                                                      | The order of the practice ladder                                                                                         |
| **Subgoal labels**         | Worked examples whose steps are labeled by purpose transfer better                                                                               | Worked examples show labeled steps                                                                                       |
| **Debugging as a skill**   | Reading and fixing unfamiliar code is a core, trainable skill                                                                                    | Debug-unfamiliar-code checks                                                                                             |
| **The notional machine**   | Novices need a working model of what each line does to the variables; tracing is the skill that builds it (du Boulay, 1986; Lister et al., 2004) | Predict-the-output questions, then Step through ([ADR-0022](decisions/0022-step-through.md)) to watch the machine's view |

## AI and learning

Unrestricted, answer-giving AI can make practice look better while hurting real learning. In a large field study, students with unrestricted GPT-4 access did better on practice problems but worse on later exams taken without AI; a tutor version with guardrails largely avoided the harm (Bastani et al., 2025). That's why our tutor only gives hints and why "prove it" checks are AI-free ([ADR-0003](decisions/0003-ai-roles-and-guardrails.md)).

## Myths we avoid

- **Learning styles.** There's no good evidence that matching teaching to "visual" or "auditory" learners helps (Pashler et al., 2008). We personalize by what you _know_, not by a preferred format.
- **Re-reading and highlighting** feel productive but do little for retention. We use retrieval instead.
- **Streaks and points.** A daily streak turns one missed day into a reason to stop, and points for their own sake can crowd out the reason for learning. Practice is counted in weeks, and progress is shown as topics proven.

## Adaptive engine background

- **Knowledge tracing** estimates what a learner knows from their answers (Corbett & Anderson, 1994). Modern variants use machine learning.
- **Knowledge space theory** models a subject as a prerequisite structure and uses it for efficient placement tests (Doignon & Falmagne; it powers ALEKS). Our placement test ([ADR-0021](decisions/0021-placement-test.md)) is the one-dimensional case: a track's topics in order are close to a chain, so what a learner knows is near enough a prefix, and finding its end is a binary search with the topic's own review questions as probes.
- **FSRS** is an open-source scheduler that predicts when you'll forget something and schedules the review just before then (Ye et al., 2022; used by Anki).
- **Implicit repetition** (Math Academy's "FIRe"): practicing an advanced topic counts as partial review of the simpler topics it uses (Skycak). Built on 2026-11-16 ([ADR-0009](decisions/0009-reviews.md#amendment-2026-11-16-implicit-repetition)): it never counts as the review Mastered needs.
