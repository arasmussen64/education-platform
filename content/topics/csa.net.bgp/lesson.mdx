<Intro>

Routing ended with static routes and the promise of routing protocols, where routers tell each other what they can reach. Inside one company that protocol is often OSPF. Between companies there is only one: **BGP**, the Border Gateway Protocol, the way the internet's networks tell each other where every address lives. It also connects you to the clouds: a VPN or a private circuit to AWS, Azure or Google Cloud runs a BGP session. This topic is BGP for a small network with two providers: how two routers start talking, what to announce and what to accept so you never leak or swallow someone else's mistake, how a router chooses one path among several, and how to choose which provider your traffic leaves by. The next topic steers the traffic coming in, and builds the clouds' circuits.

**Autonomous systems.** Nobody runs the internet. It is tens of thousands of networks (ISPs, clouds, universities, companies), each run by one organisation with one routing policy. Each is an **autonomous system**, an AS, and has a number, its **ASN**. ASNs were 16-bit numbers, up to 65,535, and are now 32-bit, up to 4,294,967,295. Some are for private use, the way 10.0.0.0/8 is for private addresses: 64512 to 65534, and 4200000000 to 4294967294 (RFC 6996). The labs use them, and so do the clouds' gateways (table below). Each AS tells its neighbours which address blocks, which prefixes, it can deliver to, and they pass the news on. That telling is BGP. A router that speaks BGP with another AS sits at the AS's border, which is where the name comes from.

**A session.** Two BGP routers, **neighbours** (or peers), talk over a TCP connection to port 179, kept open as long as they talk. Each is configured with the other's address and AS number. Picture two neighbours over a fence. First they say who they are: each sends an **OPEN** message with its AS number, its **router ID** (a number that names the router, written like an IPv4 address and usually one of its own) and the hold time it wants, and if the AS number isn't the one the other expected, the session is refused there and then (`Bad Peer AS`). Then they exchange lists: **UPDATE** messages announce prefixes (an **announcement**, or advertisement) and take them back (a **withdrawal**). Then they nod every few seconds: a **keepalive**. If one hears nothing at all for the **hold time**, it assumes the other is gone, drops the session and forgets every route it learned over it. FRR's defaults are a keepalive every 60 seconds and a hold time of 180, and it tries a failed connection again every 120 seconds; the labs use 3, 9 and 5 so that things happen in seconds. Between two ASes the session is **eBGP** (external); between two routers of one AS it's **iBGP** (internal), which carries what the AS's border routers learned to its other routers.

On its way up a session passes through states: `Idle` (configured, not trying yet, or stopped), `Connect` (opening the TCP connection), `Active` (that failed, and it's waiting to try again; a misleading name), `OpenSent` and `OpenConfirm` (OPEN messages exchanged), and `Established`. A session that sits in `Connect` or `Active` never got a TCP connection: a wrong address, no route to it, port 179 blocked, or nobody configured on the other side. One that reaches OPEN and falls back to `Idle` was refused, and `show bgp neighbors ADDRESS` says why on its `Last reset` line. In `show bgp summary`, one line per neighbour, the column `State/PfxRcd` holds the state's name while the session is down and, once it's up, the number of prefixes accepted from the neighbour (captured in the lab, ISP A's router a few seconds after a boot):

```
Neighbor        V         AS   MsgRcvd   MsgSent   TblVer  InQ OutQ  Up/Down State/PfxRcd   PfxSnt Desc
192.0.2.1       4      65001         0         0        0    0    0    never       Active        0 the shop
192.0.2.10      4      65030        15        17        0    0    0 00:00:10            2        3 transit
```

**Configuring it in FRR.** In edge's console (`on edge vtysh`, then `configure terminal`): `router bgp 65001` starts BGP for AS 65001, and `neighbor 192.0.2.2 remote-as 65010` adds a neighbour. Within seconds the session is `Established`, and then comes the surprise: both prefix columns say `(Policy) (Policy)`, and nothing moves either way. **RFC 8212** says a router must not accept routes from an eBGP neighbour, or send it any, unless an explicit policy says so, because routers that passed everything by default caused leaks. FRR follows it by default (`bgp ebgp-requires-policy`). Typing `no bgp ebgp-requires-policy` makes routes move by switching the protection off, and every lab in this topic treats it as the wrong fix it is. The right one is a policy in each direction, built from two kinds of list:

- A **prefix list** names prefixes, in numbered lines tried in order: `ip prefix-list OURS seq 5 permit 198.51.100.0/24` matches exactly that prefix. A range widens a line: `le 32` ("less than or equal to 32") adds every longer prefix inside it, so `198.51.100.0/24 le 32` is the /24 and every part of it down to single addresses, and `0.0.0.0/0 le 32` is every prefix there is.
- A **route map** is the policy itself: numbered entries, each `permit` or `deny`, each with `match` lines (which routes it applies to) and `set` lines (what to change about them). `route-map TO-ISPS permit 10` with `match ip address prefix-list OURS` lets out only the shop's prefix. A route that no entry permits is denied at the end of the map, and an entry with no `match` line matches everything.

It is applied per neighbour and direction, under `address-family ipv4 unicast`: `neighbor 192.0.2.2 route-map FROM-ISPA in` for what edge accepts, `... TO-ISPS out` for what it sends. `end` finishes; `write memory` saves, as in Routing.

**What you announce.** `network 198.51.100.0/24` announces a prefix, but only if the routing table holds exactly that prefix: the shop's connected /24 is there, so it goes out. A typo, `network 198.51.100.0/25`, announces nothing, silently. Announce only your own prefixes. A network that passes one provider's routes on to another has told the second "you can reach the first one's customers through me": a **route leak**, and in the lab it was visible at once in ISP B's looking glass, ISP A's customers' prefix arriving from the shop with the AS path `65001 65010`. Networks stand in three kinds of relationship, and they decide what to pass on: a **customer** pays a **provider** for **transit**, carrying its traffic to the rest of the internet; two **peers** exchange their own and their customers' traffic for free. A provider tells its customers everything and the world about its customers; a customer tells its providers only about itself. The shop is a customer of both ISPs, so it should carry traffic for nobody but itself.

**What you accept.** Refuse your own prefix and anything inside it: `ip prefix-list NOT-OURS seq 5 deny 198.51.100.0/24 le 32`, then `seq 10 permit 0.0.0.0/0 le 32` for everything else. If a provider sends 198.51.100.128/25, half the shop's own network, longest-prefix match (from Routing) makes it beat the shop's connected /24, and the router sends its own website's traffic away. In the lab it bounced between edge and ISP B until its TTL ran out, and the website was unreachable from outside while the office, on the same LAN, still reached it. Sent by mistake it's a leak; sent on purpose to steal traffic it's a **prefix hijack**. **RPKI** (Resource Public Key Infrastructure) lets the holder of an address block publish, signed, which AS may originate it, so routers that check can refuse anyone else's announcement; a small network's first defence is still its own filter. The last fuse is a limit: `neighbor ADDRESS maximum-prefix 1000` tears the session down if the neighbour suddenly sends more than 1,000 prefixes, better than taking a million wrong ones. The clouds' gateways keep such a limit on your side of the session:

| | AWS (VPN on a virtual private gateway) | Azure (VPN Gateway) | Google Cloud (Cloud Router) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The cloud side's AS number | 64512 unless you choose another, fixed once created | 65515 unless you choose another (65515 and 65517 to 65520 are kept for Azure) | a private ASN you choose (16550 with Partner Interconnect) |
| Prefixes it takes from you | 100, not adjustable | 4,000; more, and the session is dropped | 5,000 from one peer; more, and the session is reset |
| Timers | not stated as a default on its pages | keepalive 60 s, hold 180 s, fixed | keepalive 20 s by default (20 to 60), hold three times that |

(Their pages, read 2026-11-12.) Each also takes your side's AS number, public or private.

**Two real incidents.** On 24 June 2019 a small ISP's "BGP optimizer" made more specific routes for parts of other networks' prefixes. Its customer passed them on to its other provider, Verizon, which accepted them and passed them to the rest of the internet; more specific routes win, so traffic for large parts of the internet tried to squeeze through a small company. Two filters were missing: the customer's export filter, and Verizon's filter on what its customer sent. Cloudflare's account, below, is worth reading with one question: which filter, on which router, would have stopped it? On 4 October 2021 Facebook's network withdrew its own routes, among them those of its DNS servers (the name servers that turn facebook.com into addresses), and Facebook vanished from the internet for hours (an optional reading): a withdrawal travels as fast as an announcement.

**How a router chooses.** Each path a router hears for a prefix comes with **path attributes**: the **AS path** (the ASes it crossed, nearest first, which is also how BGP avoids loops: a router ignores a path that already holds its own AS), the **next hop** (where to send packets), the **origin** (`i` for a prefix announced with `network`, `?` for one copied in from another source), and optional ones below. BGP compares the paths one attribute at a time and stops at the first difference (FRR 8.4's order):

1. highest **weight**: FRR's own, kept on this one router, never sent anywhere (32768 for routes the router announces itself);
2. highest **local preference** (default 100): set by the AS's own policy, shared with its other routers over iBGP;
3. a route this router announces itself;
4. shortest AS path;
5. origin: `i` before `?`;
6. lowest **MED** (multi-exit discriminator, a hint a neighbouring AS sends about which of its links to use), compared only between paths from the same neighbouring AS;
7. eBGP over iBGP;
8. the nearest next hop inside the AS, then tie-breaks: the path chosen already (for stability), the lowest router ID, the lowest neighbour address.

Here is edge's table for the cloud's network before and after the shop preferred ISP A (captured, columns trimmed):

```
   Network          Next Hop            LocPrf Path
*> 203.0.113.0/24   192.0.2.6                  65020 65100 i
*                   192.0.2.2                  65010 65030 65100 i

*  203.0.113.0/24   192.0.2.6                  65020 65100 i
*>                  192.0.2.2              200 65010 65030 65100 i
```

`*` is a valid path and `>` the chosen one; a prefix is printed once, so a second path's line starts with its next hop. First both have the default local preference (an empty `LocPrf` means 100), so the shorter AS path wins: two networks through ISP B against three through ISP A (FRR: `best (AS Path)`). Then ISP A's paths carry 200, and local preference is compared before AS path length (`best (Local Pref)`). The kernel installs only the winner, so `ip route get` follows it. When two paths tie to the end, FRR keeps the one it chose first, which depends on which session came up first and can change after a restart. The labs' far routers carry `bgp bestpath compare-routerid` (settle a tie by the lowest router ID instead), and the shop's two paths differ in length, so no lab is ever decided by time.

**Choosing your way out: local preference.** ISP A is the cheaper contract. Add `set local-preference 200` to the route map that ISP A's routes come in through, and every route from ISP A beats ISP B's, however long its AS path; in a company with several border routers, iBGP carries the 200 to all of them, so they all agree (FRR's weight would have changed one router only). Confirm it three ways: `show bgp ipv4 unicast PREFIX` (the `LocPrf` and the `>`), `ip route get ADDRESS` (what the kernel will do), and traceroute from the office. Then look at the other direction. Local preference never leaves the AS, so nobody else's choice has changed: in the lab, the cloud's looking glass still showed its best path to the shop through ISP B, and replies came back that way, an asymmetric path, as in Routing. How traffic comes in depends on what other networks choose from the routes you announce, and steering it is the next topic. When ISP A's session drops (at once when its cable goes; after the hold time when the path dies silently) its routes are withdrawn, ISP B's are the best left, and traffic moves there by itself, and back when the session returns.

**Looking from outside.** Many real networks publish a **looking glass**, a web page where anyone can run a `show bgp` command on their routers, to see what the rest of the internet hears about you. The labs give you one for every router that isn't yours: `looking-glass ispb 'show bgp ipv4 unicast neighbors 192.0.2.5 routes'` lists what ISP B accepted from the shop, the first place to look for a leak. The far routers are scenery, and the checks notice if anyone changes them.

<Recap>

- **An AS** is one organisation's network with one routing policy, numbered (private ranges 64512 to 65534 and 4200000000 to 4294967294). **BGP** is how ASes tell each other which prefixes they reach: **eBGP** between ASes, **iBGP** inside one.
- **A session:** TCP port 179, each side configured with the other's address and AS; OPEN (a wrong AS is refused: `Bad Peer AS`), UPDATEs that announce and withdraw, keepalives; silence for the hold time drops it and its routes. FRR: 60 and 180 seconds, retry every 120; the labs: 3, 9 and 5.
- **States:** `Idle`, `Connect`, `Active` (trying and failing to get TCP up), `OpenSent`, `OpenConfirm`, `Established`. `show bgp summary` shows a state's name, or a prefix count once up; `show bgp neighbors ADDRESS` has the `Last reset` reason.
- **FRR:** `router bgp ASN`, `neighbor ADDRESS remote-as ASN`, then under `address-family ipv4 unicast`: `network PREFIX` and `neighbor ADDRESS route-map NAME in|out`. **RFC 8212**: no policy, no routes, `(Policy) (Policy)`; the fix is a route map each way, never `no bgp ebgp-requires-policy`.
- **Prefix lists** (`permit|deny PREFIX [le N]`) name prefixes; **route maps** (`permit|deny N`, `match`, `set`) are the policy, with a deny at the end.
- **Announce** only your own prefix (`network` needs it in the routing table). Passing one provider's routes to another is a **route leak**. **Refuse** your own prefix `le 32` from every provider, or a more specific route (a mistake or a **hijack**) beats your own. **RPKI** lets routers check who may originate a prefix. **`maximum-prefix`** is a fuse; the clouds keep one too (AWS 100, Azure 4,000, Google 5,000).
- **Choosing a path:** weight (one router's own), local preference (highest), own route, AS path (shortest), origin, MED (lowest, from one neighbouring AS only), eBGP over iBGP, tie-breaks. `>` marks the winner; the kernel installs only it.
- **Your way out:** `set local-preference 200` in the inbound route map from the preferred provider; shared over iBGP; check with `show bgp`, `ip route get` and traceroute. It changes nothing about how others reach you. A dropped session withdraws its routes and the backup takes over.
- **`looking-glass NAME 'show ...'`** reads a router that isn't yours, as real networks' looking glasses let anyone do.

</Recap>
