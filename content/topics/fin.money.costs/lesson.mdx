Compound interest showed growth building on itself. Costs do the same thing in reverse. This topic computes what a yearly fee and taxes take over decades, and compares two options fairly. It teaches how these costs work; it isn't advice on what to buy, and the taxes in it are a made-up flat rate, not any country's rules.

**Two kinds of fee.** A **transaction fee** is charged when you buy or sell: a commission, or a charge for getting into a fund. An **ongoing fee** is charged every year whatever you do, as a share of what you hold. A fund's yearly running costs are stated as its **expense ratio**, a percentage of the money in it, and they come out of the fund before you see its return. A 1% charge taken once costs you 1%. A 1% charge taken every year costs you far more, because it's taken again and again on a balance that includes everything earlier years grew.

<Check item="contrast-yearly-or-one-off-fee" />

**How a yearly fee compounds.** This topic uses one rule, the rule whose numbers match the US Securities and Exchange Commission's own fee example: each year the balance grows, then the fee is taken as a share of the new balance. In code:

```python
for year in range(years):
    balance = balance * (1 + growth)
    balance = balance * (1 - fee)
```

Without the fee the money is multiplied by `(1 + growth) ** years`; with it, by that times `(1 - fee) ** years`. So the share of the no-fee amount that the fee takes, the **fee drag**, is `1 - (1 - fee) ** years`, and under this rule it doesn't depend on the growth rate at all. It does depend on time: a 1% fee takes about 10% over 10 years, 18% over 20, 26% over 30 and 33% over 40. (Some calculators subtract the fee from the growth rate instead, `(1 + growth - fee) ** years`; the answers differ a little, so a fair comparison uses one rule for both options.)

**Comparing two options.** To compare, work out both final amounts for the same start, growth and years, then give the difference as a share of the better one: `(better - worse) / better`. Dividing by the worse one gives a bigger number that answers a different question ("how much more does the better one have?"). Growth is uncertain and nobody can choose it; a fee is known in advance and is one of the few parts of the return an investor does choose.

<Check item="mcq-why-costs-are-chosen" />

**Taxes, in outline.** Taxes on investment gains compound in the same way as fees. Take a made-up flat tax of 25% on gains. In an ordinary account taxed every year, a 6% gain becomes 4.5% after tax, the **after-tax return**, and the balance compounds at 4.5%. In a **tax-deferred account**, the gains aren't taxed as they happen; in this topic's outline they're taxed once, at the same 25%, when the money comes out at the end. The deferred account ends with more, because the money that would have gone in tax each year stays invested and keeps growing until the end. On $10,000 over 30 years that's about $37,450 against $45,580. Real accounts' rules differ by country and by account: some tax the whole withdrawal, some tax nothing, rates change. The point here is only the shape: paying later lets more money compound for longer.
