<Intro>

BGP sessions and policy ended with your way out: local preference on the routes from the provider you prefer, and the warning that it changes nothing about how others reach you. This topic is the rest of running BGP to a cloud. How to steer the traffic coming in, which you don't control. What happens when one company has several routers that run BGP, and why they can't simply pass each other what they hear. How fast a failed link is noticed, and how to make it fast. And how the three clouds' private circuits, AWS Direct Connect, Azure ExpressRoute and Google Cloud Interconnect, use all of it, each with its own rules.

**Two directions, two deciders.** Picture your router and the cloud's router at the two ends of two circuits. Every router chooses its own way out, so your router decides which circuit your packets leave by: that is **outbound traffic engineering**, and local preference does it. The cloud's router decides which circuit its packets come back by, from the paths it has heard for your prefix and its own policy: that is **inbound traffic engineering**, and you can't set anything on the cloud's router. All you can change is what you announce to it. Keep the two arrows apart, as in Routing's "the way back is its own route": a company that sets local preference and thinks it has chosen a primary circuit has chosen half of one.

**The tools for the way in, strongest first.**

1. **A more specific prefix.** Announce 10.1.0.0/23 over both circuits and 10.1.0.0/24 over circuit 1 only, and the cloud sends traffic for the /24 over circuit 1 whatever its preferences, because the two are different prefixes and the longest match wins before any attribute is compared. It works everywhere, and it costs prefixes: every extra one counts against the provider's limit, and it must not leak to the internet.
2. **A community the provider documents.** A **community** is a label on a route, written `ASN:VALUE` (a 32-bit number, by custom the AS number of the network that defined the label, then a value). It means only what that network says it means. AWS defines `7224:7100`, `7224:7200` and `7224:7300` as low, medium and high local preference on its side: you can't set the cloud's local preference, but where the cloud has promised to set it from your labels, you can ask. 7224 is AWS's own public AS number, whatever private number its side of your session uses.
3. **AS-path prepending.** Add your own AS number to the path again on the backup, `set as-path prepend 65001 65001`, so that path reads `65001 65001 65001` and looks three networks long. A shorter path wins, but only once local preference is equal, so prepending loses against any provider that decides by local preference first.
4. **MED** (multi-exit discriminator), named in BGP sessions and policy: a number you send one neighbour, lower preferred, saying which of your links to it you'd like it to use. It is compared only between paths from the same neighbouring AS, and late in the order, after local preference and AS path.

The walkthrough tries the last three in turn against a cloud modelled on AWS, and the reason FRR gives for each choice, `best (...)`, shows the order at work: MED 50 against 100 moves nothing (`best (Local Pref)`, because the cloud gives circuit 2 a preference of 200 and circuit 1 150), a double prepend moves nothing either (local preference again), and the community `7224:7300` moves the cloud's traffic at once (captured):

```
  65001
    169.254.30.1 from 169.254.30.1 (10.1.0.1)
      Origin IGP, metric 0, localpref 300, valid, external, best (Local Pref)
      Community: 7224:7300
  65001
    169.254.20.1 from 169.254.20.1 (10.1.0.1)
      Origin IGP, metric 0, localpref 100, valid, external
      Community: 7224:7100
```

In FRR a community is set in an outbound route map, `set community 7224:7300`, and FRR sends communities by default. To act on communities you receive, a router names them in a list, `bgp community-list standard HIGH seq 5 permit 7224:7300`, and matches it in a route map, `match community HIGH`: that is exactly how the lab's cloud is configured (`looking-glass cloud 'show running-config'`). A few communities mean the same to every router (RFC 1997): **NO_EXPORT**, "use this, but pass it to no other AS", and **NO_ADVERTISE**, "pass it to nobody". AWS tags every route it sends you over a public virtual interface with NO_EXPORT.

**Equal paths are shared.** When two paths tie on everything, FRR marks both `multipath` and installs both: the kernel's route shows two next hops, and each connection takes one of them (ECMP, from Routing). AWS documents the same for virtual interfaces whose paths are equal. That is why the labs' checks read the kernel's whole route, `ip route show PREFIX`, and not only BGP's `best`: a tie can look like a choice in `show bgp` and still send half the traffic the wrong way.

**The clouds, side by side.** Each cloud has its own words. On AWS, a Direct Connect **connection** is the physical circuit, and a **virtual interface** (VIF) is a BGP session over it: **private** to reach your VPCs, **transit** to reach a Transit Gateway, **public** to reach AWS's public services. Every Direct Connect location is tied to one AWS Region, its **associated Region**. On Azure, an ExpressRoute **circuit** is always two links, primary and secondary, to Microsoft's edge routers (**MSEE**s), with a BGP session on each; **private peering** reaches your virtual networks and **Microsoft peering** Microsoft's public services. On Google Cloud, Cloud Interconnect is **Dedicated** (your own circuit) or **Partner** (through a provider), each circuit is split into **VLAN attachments**, and your BGP sessions are with a **Cloud Router**, which only exchanges routes and forwards nothing. Their documentation, read 2026-11-12:

| | AWS Direct Connect | Azure ExpressRoute | Google Cloud Interconnect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Its AS number | 7224 on public VIFs; on private ones, your gateway's private ASN (64512 unless you chose another) | 12076 | your Cloud Router's private ASN; 16550 with Partner Interconnect |
| Redundancy | separate connections on separate devices, at more than one location for the most resilience | two BGP sessions per peering, both required for the SLA | 99.99% takes four VLAN attachments in two metros and two Cloud Routers |
| Prefixes it takes from you | 100 on a private or transit VIF by default (up to 1,000), and more sends the session idle; 1,000 on public | 4,000 on private peering (10,000 with Premium); 200 per session on Microsoft peering; more and the session drops | more than 5,000 from one peer resets the session; beyond its quota, prefixes are dropped |
| Making its traffic come to you over one circuit | longest prefix, then your communities 7224:7100/7200/7300, then AS path, then MED, which AWS "does not recommend" | longest prefix, then AS path: more specifics or prepending; "Microsoft doesn't honor any community values" you send | it compares your routes by MED and, in its standard best-path mode, AS path (its default, legacy, doesn't use AS path across Cloud Routers) |
| Its preference, told to you | on public VIFs, communities on its routes (7224:8100 for your associated Region, 7224:8200 for its continent); otherwise your local preference decides alone | regional communities on its routes, such as 12076:50004 for East US, to set your local preference by | MED: the "advertised route priority", base 100, higher for other regions' subnets |
| BGP timers and BFD | hold 90 s, keepalive 30 s; BFD down to 300 ms x 3, on by default on AWS's side | its routers use 60 and 180 s; BFD on by default on new peerings, 300 ms (sometimes 750) | BFD off by default; 1,000 ms x 5 once on |

Two subtleties decide real designs. On AWS, with no community, a location in the associated Region gets medium local preference and others "a lower value", so a backup in the associated Region beats a primary outside it however much you prepend: only a community fixes that (the walkthrough's cloud models it, 200 against 150, and AWS doesn't say how much lower; the lab uses 150). On Azure, the virtual network's choice of circuit can also be set by a **connection weight**, which "is considered before the AS Path length". Microsoft names neither MED nor communities as tools for its traffic to you, so the write lab's Azure-style cloud ignores both.

**One AS, many routers: route reflection.** A company with several routers that run BGP joins them with iBGP, and iBGP has a rule that surprises everyone once: **a router never passes a route it learned over iBGP to another iBGP neighbour**. The reason is loops. eBGP catches a loop with the AS path (a router ignores a path that already holds its own AS), but inside one AS the path doesn't change from router to router, so iBGP stops loops by not passing routes on at all. Then every router must peer with every other, a **full mesh**: n(n - 1)/2 sessions for n routers, 6 for 4, 45 for 10, 190 for 20. In the fill-in's data centre the cloud's routes reach dc1 and dc2 and stop at core (captured, two of core's three neighbours; `PfxSnt` is what core sends each):

```
Neighbor        V         AS   MsgRcvd   MsgSent   TblVer  InQ OutQ  Up/Down State/PfxRcd   PfxSnt Desc
10.0.1.2        4      65001        87       107        0    0    0 00:00:22            4        0 access
10.0.2.2        4      65001        94       107        0    0    0 00:00:10            1        0 dc1
```

A **route reflector** is a router allowed to break the rule for its **clients**: `neighbor 10.0.1.2 route-reflector-client` on core. RFC 4456 gives the reflector's two rules: a route from a client goes to all the other peers, clients or not; a route from a non-client goes to the clients. Only routes between two non-clients still stop. To stop loops, the reflector adds two marks to each route it passes on, the **originator** (the router ID of the router that first brought the route into the AS) and the **cluster list** (the reflectors it has passed through), and a router that finds its own ID in either drops the route. access then sees the cloud's route (captured):

```
    10.0.2.2 from 10.0.1.1 (169.254.30.1)
      Origin IGP, metric 0, localpref 200, valid, internal, best (First path received)
      Originator: 169.254.30.1, Cluster list: 10.0.1.1
```

Real networks run two reflectors, each client peering with both, so one can fail: 2 x 10 + 1 = 21 sessions for twelve routers instead of the mesh's 66. **The next-hop trap** comes with it. A route learned over eBGP keeps the far side's address as its next hop, here the cloud's address on dc1's circuit, 169.254.30.2, and a reflector doesn't change it. If nothing inside the AS has a route to that cable, the route shows `inaccessible` and is never used. The border router fixes it with `neighbor CORE next-hop-self`, which puts its own address there; carrying routes to the circuits inside the AS also works.

**Announcing a summary.** A provider's prefix limit is a fuse, as in BGP sessions and policy, and here it bites your own design: the build's office has eight /24s and the cloud takes 5 per session. Eight networks side by side, 10.1.0.0/24 to 10.1.7.0/24, make one /21 (three bits, 2 x 2 x 2 = 8, from CIDR). In FRR, `network` lines put the eight into BGP, and `aggregate-address 10.1.0.0/21 summary-only` announces the summary and holds the eight back. Send more than the limit and the cloud tears the session down at once; its summary shows `Idle (PfxCt)` and its neighbour details "will restart in 56 seconds" (captured). Nothing passes in either direction until then.

**Noticing failure.** A cable that loses its carrier is a **loud** failure: both routers see the interface go down and drop its routes at once (one ping lost in the walkthrough). A path that dies while the interfaces stay up, a fault in a provider's equipment, is **silent**, and a static route never notices it (Routing). BGP does, but only when no keepalive has arrived for the hold time: 9 seconds in the labs, 90 by default on AWS's side, 180 on Microsoft's. In the walkthrough, 53 of 75 pings sent every 0.2 seconds were lost, about 10 seconds, and the session's `Last reset` said `Hold Timer Expired`. Traffic is lost for all of it.

**BFD** (bidirectional forwarding detection) is the fix. Two routers send each other a tiny packet every **interval** and declare the path down when a **multiplier**'s worth of intervals pass with nothing heard: the detection time is interval x multiplier. FRR's defaults and AWS's minimums are 300 ms x 3 = 900 ms, and Microsoft's edge runs at 300 ms (its example sets a multiplier of 3); Google's defaults are 1,000 ms x 5 = 5 seconds. The two sides agree on the slower setting, so a 3,000 ms profile on your side makes 9 seconds, no better than the hold time. When BFD declares the path down, BGP drops the session at once: the walkthrough's silent failure cost 5 pings, one second. In FRR, `bfdd=yes` in `/etc/frr/daemons` runs the BFD daemon, `neighbor ADDRESS bfd` puts a session under it, a `bfd` section can hold a named `profile` with other values, and `show bfd peers` shows each side's timers. **Graceful restart** is the opposite idea: a router that restarts asks its neighbour to keep using its routes meanwhile, which works against noticing a failure fast, and AWS recommends not using the two together.

**Designing two circuits and a VPN.** Outbound, local preference in three steps: highest on the routes from the primary circuit, then the backup, then the VPN, so each takes over only when the ones above it are gone. Leave the middle step out and the backup and the VPN tie, and the tie decides: in the build it goes to the VPN, so traffic would cross the internet as soon as the primary failed, with the backup circuit idle. Inbound, whatever the provider honours: AWS's communities, prepending or more specifics on Azure, and on Google, which tells you its own preference, MED. AWS already prefers Direct Connect routes to VPN routes for the same prefix (Routing's priority list), and the build's cloud does the same. Summarise to stay under the limit, and put BFD on both circuits. A real circuit takes weeks to deliver and bills every month, so this topic models them with FRR, each cloud labelled "modelled on" and quoted; Module 11 builds a VPN with BGP to a real cloud.

<Recap>

- **Two directions, two deciders.** Your way out is yours: local preference. The way in is the far side's, from what you announce: you can only influence it.
- **The way in, strongest first:** a **more specific prefix** (longest match beats every attribute); a **community** the provider documents (AWS's `7224:7100`, `7200`, `7300` become its local preference, compared before AS path); **AS-path prepending** (`set as-path prepend`, loses to the far side's local preference); **MED** (one neighbour, late in the order).
- **Communities:** `ASN:VALUE` labels that mean what their owner says; `set community`, `bgp community-list`, `match community`; **NO_EXPORT** (no other AS) and **NO_ADVERTISE** (nobody).
- **Ties are shared:** equal paths are both installed (ECMP); read the kernel's whole route, not only BGP's `best`.
- **The clouds:** AWS honours its communities and prefers its associated Region; Azure honours no community you send (prepend or more specifics) and tells you its regions by community; Google tells you its preference as MED (base 100). Limits: AWS 100 per private VIF (idle above it), Azure 4,000 on private peering (dropped).
- **Route reflection:** iBGP never passes iBGP routes on, so a full mesh needs n(n - 1)/2 sessions; a **route reflector** passes routes for its **clients** (`route-reflector-client`), marking **originator** and **cluster list**; run two; the border sets **next-hop-self**, or the reflected route's next hop is `inaccessible`.
- **Summarise** with `aggregate-address PREFIX summary-only`, or a provider's limit takes the session down (`Idle (PfxCt)`).
- **Failure:** loud (carrier lost) at once; silent only after the hold time (AWS 90 s, Microsoft 180 s, FRR 180 s, the labs 9 s). **BFD** detects in interval x multiplier: 300 ms x 3 = 0.9 s; Google 1,000 ms x 5, off by default; the slower side's setting wins. `bfdd=yes`, `neighbor ADDRESS bfd`, `show bfd peers`.
- **Two circuits and a VPN:** local preference primary over backup over VPN; inbound by the provider's own rules; summarise; BFD on both circuits.

</Recap>
