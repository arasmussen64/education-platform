The last topic put a sign-in on the wire: tokens, claims, the realm file, the scripted browser. This one asks a harder question than "who are you". It asks "how sure are we, and can that be faked". You will watch a password and a code be stolen by a site in the middle, see a security key shrug off the same attack, require a key for the people who need it, read from a token which methods a sign-in actually used, write the if-then rules that decide a sign-in's fate, keep an emergency way in that doesn't quietly become the weak spot, and turn it all on without locking anyone out.

## A phishing site that beats MFA

**Phishing** is tricking a person into handing a secret to an attacker's site dressed up as the real one, usually through a link in an email or a text. The classic defence is a second factor: even if the password leaks, the attacker lacks the code. That defence has a hole, and the hole has a name.

An **adversary-in-the-middle relay** (AiTM) is a phishing site that does not just collect what you type and stop. It sits between you and the real site and passes everything through in real time. You see the real sign-in page, because the relay fetched it live. You type your password; the relay types it into the real site. The real site asks for your code; the relay shows you that page; you type the code; the relay types it in, well within the half-minute it is good for. The real site is convinced, and hands back a **session cookie**, the small value a site sets after a sign-in that stands in for the sign-in from then on. The relay keeps that cookie. Now the attacker holds a signed-in session: no password, no code, just the cookie, until it expires.

Everything the person did was correct. They used a strong password and a real second factor. The weakness is not the person and not the code; it is that both are things you type, and anything you type into a page can be read by whoever controls that page. The padlock does not save you: a certificate proves you reached the name in the address bar privately, and an attacker can register a lookalike name (`login.shop-help.test`) and get a perfectly real certificate for it. The padlock is green and the page is a trap.

One-time codes come in a few shapes, and a relay beats them all. A **one-time password** (OTP) is a short code good once or for a few seconds. The common kind is **TOTP** (time-based): your app and the server share a secret, and both turn it and the current clock into a six-digit code that changes every 30 seconds. A **push notification** sends a prompt to your phone that you approve with a tap; **number matching** makes you type a number shown on screen, which stops you approving a prompt you didn't start (push bombing) but does nothing against a relay that shows you the right number. A code sent by text message adds its own weaknesses: a **SIM swap**, where an attacker moves your number to their SIM, delivers your codes to them. CISA ranks these methods, strongest to weakest: FIDO/WebAuthn and smart cards at the top, then app codes and push with number matching ("vulnerable to phishing"), then push alone, then SMS and voice.

<Story title="Cloudflare, July 2022: the keys that didn't care about the relay" source="https://blog.cloudflare.com/2022-07-sms-phishing-attacks/" sourceTitle="The mechanics of a sophisticated phishing scam and how we stopped it" license="linked, not copied">

On the evening of 20 July 2022, text messages went out to dozens of Cloudflare staff and some of their families, pointing at an official-looking address, `cloudflare-okta.com`, registered less than an hour before. The page was a pixel copy of the company's real sign-in. Three employees fell for it and typed their usernames and passwords, which the phishing page relayed to the attacker in real time.

And the attackers got nowhere. Cloudflare does not use one-time codes at all; every employee signs in with a FIDO2 security key. The stolen passwords were correct, and the logins were stopped cold at the key step. Cloudflare's write-up credits the keys being "tied to users" and implementing origin binding: the key will not answer for a site that is not the one it was set up for, so the real password in the attacker's hands opened nothing. No Cloudflare systems were touched.

It is the puzzle's story with the ending changed. The same relay, the same stolen password; a different second factor, and a different outcome.

## Why a security key can't be relayed

A **security key** is a small device (or a chip built into your laptop or phone) that holds a private key and signs a challenge to prove you are there. **FIDO2** is the standard behind it, and **WebAuthn** is the part your browser and the server speak. Here is the whole idea in one picture.

When you register a key with a site, the key makes a new keypair just for that site's name and hands the site the public half. The site's name is the **relying party ID** (RP ID), a host name like `login.shop.test`. At each later sign-in the site sends a fresh random **challenge**; your browser wraps it together with the **origin** it is really on (the scheme, host and port in the address bar, `https://login.shop.test`), and the key signs the bundle with the private half it keeps. The site checks the signature with the public half it stored, and checks that the origin inside it is the site's own.

Now put that key in front of the relay. The relay's page is served from `login.shop-help.test`. Two things go wrong for the attacker, each at a different place:

- The **browser** will ask the key only for its own site's name. A page on `login.shop-help.test` cannot ask for a key registered to `login.shop.test`; the browser refuses before the key is even touched.
- The **key** holds a credential only for the name it registered with. Even if the browser were tricked, the key has nothing for the lookalike.
- And if both of those were somehow bypassed, the **server** checks the origin inside what was signed, and refuses a signature made for the wrong site.

There is nothing to type, so there is nothing for the relay to collect, and the one signature that is made is bound to the real site's name and useless anywhere else. That binding is why FIDO2 keys, passkeys and smart-card certificates are **phishing-resistant**: a method a relay cannot defeat, because the secret never leaves the device and the proof is tied to the real site. Passwords, one-time codes and push are not phishing-resistant, because they carry no such binding.

A **passkey** is a WebAuthn credential that can stand in for the password entirely. A device-bound passkey, such as a hardware security key, keeps its private key on the one device and cannot be copied off it. A synced passkey is copied between your devices through a cloud account, which is convenient and recoverable but means the key is exportable by design.

That distinction decides how strong a sign-in counts as. NIST's guidance (SP 800-63B) sets three **authenticator assurance levels**. AAL1 is the floor. At **AAL2**, verifiers "SHALL offer at least one phishing-resistant authentication option". **AAL3** is stricter: it requires a phishing-resistant authenticator whose private key cannot be exported, so a synced passkey, whose key is exportable, "SHALL NOT be used at AAL3", while a device-bound key qualifies. In plain terms: synced passkeys are phishing-resistant and fine for most things; a regulator who demands AAL3 wants a key that never leaves its hardware.

NIST names the two ways a protocol can be phishing-resistant: **channel binding** (tying the proof to the encrypted connection, as client-authenticated TLS does) and **verifier name binding** (tying it to the verifier's name, as WebAuthn does). You do not need the cryptography; you need the consequence, which is that the proof is worthless to a site that is not the real one.

One soft spot remains: **registration**. A key is only as trustworthy as the moment it was enrolled. If someone can add their first key after typing only a password, then a phisher who has the password can add *their own* key and pass every phishing-resistant check from then on. So the first key must be enrolled under a stronger proof: Microsoft Entra issues a short-lived **Temporary Access Pass** (TAP) for exactly this, so a new person can register a key without a weaker standing method. The key is strong; the door you let it in through must be too.

## Offering a key isn't requiring it

Giving people a key changes nothing on its own. If a sign-in can still be finished with a password and a code, the phisher simply steers the admin to the code, and the key sits unused. The rule is: **enforce the strength, don't just offer it.** While any phishable method still finishes an admin's sign-in, the phisher picks that one.

An identity provider decides what a sign-in must pass with a **flow**: a short tree of steps, each marked `REQUIRED`, `ALTERNATIVE`, `CONDITIONAL` or `DISABLED`. The shop's flow asks for a password, and then, *if the person is an admin*, a security key as a must; everyone else gives an app code. In the labs you write that as two conditional sub-flows: one whose condition is "has the admin role" and whose step is a key `REQUIRED`, and one whose condition is "does not have the admin role" and whose step is the code.

Two ways it goes wrong, and you will meet both:

- **The key as an alternative, not a must.** If the key sits beside the code as `ALTERNATIVE`, an admin can finish with the code, and the key is decoration. (On the lab's provider a lone `ALTERNATIVE` key with no other option acts like a must, because the provider then drives key registration; the weakening version is the one that leaves a phishable path open beside it.)
- **A condition that matches nobody.** If the admin sub-flow's condition names a role no one actually holds (`admins` where the real role is `admin`), it never fires, the whole sub-flow is skipped, and admins sign in on whatever is left, usually a password alone. Nothing crashes and nothing is blocked; the gate is there and opens for everyone. This is "failing open", and only a test with a real admin shows it.

Once the flow is right, how does an app, or an auditor, know a given sign-in used a key rather than a code? The token says so, in the **`amr`** claim ("authentication methods references"): a list such as `["pwd", "hwk"]` (password and a hardware key) or `["pwd", "otp"]` (password and an app code). The provider fills it in from the steps the sign-in passed, if its flow is set up to record them. An app can read `amr` to decide what to allow; a check can read it to prove the flow is really doing its job.

## Conditional access: if-then for sign-ins

Requiring a key by role is one rule. Real organisations want many: this app needs a compliant device, that one is off-limits from risky sign-ins, legacy protocols are banned outright. Microsoft Entra's engine for this is **Conditional Access**, and its policies are, in Microsoft's own words, "if-then statements": *if* this user (or group, or role), for this app, under these conditions, *then* block, or grant only with these controls. They are evaluated after the first factor, at every sign-in.

A policy has three parts. Its **assignments and conditions** say when it applies: which users, which apps, and which **signals** (the network location, the device's state, the sign-in's risk, the client type). Its **grant controls** say what it then demands: a **grant control** is `block`, or a requirement like multifactor authentication, an **authentication strength** (a named bar such as "phishing-resistant MFA"), or a **compliant device**.

Several policies can apply to one sign-in, and they combine by a few fixed rules. Learn these; they are the whole game:

- **Which policies apply.** Every condition a policy sets must match. An **exclusion beats an inclusion**: a person caught by both an include and an exclude is left out. This is how a dedicated group carries emergency accounts safely past a policy, and how a careless exclude group waves ordinary people through.
- **What they demand.** If any applicable policy says **block**, the sign-in is blocked, and no grant can undo it: a block wins. Otherwise every applicable policy's controls must be met: within one policy you can require all of its controls (AND) or any one of them (OR); across policies it is always all of them.

The signals are worth naming. **Device compliance** means a device is registered with the organisation and a management service (Microsoft's is Intune) reports it healthy: up to date, encrypted, locked; in a browser it proves which device it is with a client certificate, the same idea as the certificates in the PKI topic. **Sign-in risk** is the provider's live judgement that a particular sign-in may not be the real person (an odd location, a known-bad address); **user risk** is the same judgement about the account over time. **Report-only** is a fourth state a policy can be in, covered below.

## Break glass: a way in that doesn't weaken anything

Require a key for admins, block risky sign-ins, demand compliant devices, and one day the whole thing turns against you: the identity provider is down, the MFA service is out, the phone network is dead, the last admin has left, or a policy you just saved locks everyone out including yourself. A **break-glass account** (emergency access account) is the account you keep for that day. Microsoft's guidance is specific, and every part has a reason:

- **Two or more, cloud-only.** Two, so one lost key is not a disaster. Cloud-only (on the provider's own domain, not federated from elsewhere), so they work when the thing that federates them is the thing that is down.
- **A phishing-resistant key, different from the daily method.** A passkey or certificate, kept somewhere safe. Different from what the admins use every day, because if the daily method is what failed or got phished, an emergency account that shares it is no help. And mandatory MFA applies to these accounts too, so a long password alone is not allowed.
- **Excluded only from the policies that block or restrict, through one dedicated group.** "Excluded" is not "unprotected": the account still has its strong key and is watched. You exclude it just from the policies that could stop it getting in, and you do it through a single group whose only members are the emergency accounts, so a later edit cannot slip an ordinary admin in. Report-only policies need no exclusion, because they never enforce.
- **An alert on every sign-in, and a drill.** These accounts should almost never sign in, so every use is worth an alert someone reads: a real use means a real outage or an intrusion. And test them on a schedule (Microsoft says at least every 90 days, and after staff changes), because a credential kept for the worst day is worthless if it has quietly expired.

The hard part is proving the exclusion didn't quietly weaken everything else. The test is two-sided: the emergency account gets in during a simulated outage, *and* a made-up ordinary person in another group is still held to every policy. If both hold, the door is open for emergencies and no one else slipped through it.

## Rolling out without locking yourself out

A new sign-in rule can lock out a crowd you didn't expect. The tools for turning one on safely are all about seeing before enforcing.

**Report-only** is a policy state in which the policy is worked out at every sign-in and written to the log, but never enforced: nobody is prompted or blocked. The log records "Report-only: Success" or "Report-only: Failure" (a sign-in it would have blocked) or "User action required" (one it would have prompted). You run a new block policy in report-only for a week, read how many "Failure" lines it drew, and only then decide. The **What If** tool does the same for one imagined sign-in: you describe a user, app and conditions, and it tells you which policies would apply and, for each that wouldn't, the first condition that didn't match. Microsoft's own lockout warning is to apply a sweeping policy to a small group first and keep at least one admin excluded until it is proven.

A few numbers are worth carrying. A TOTP code changes every 30 seconds, and servers usually accept the step before and the step after as well, so a single code can be good for about 90 seconds, long enough for any relay. Guessing is not the threat: six digits is a million codes, and five tries before a lockout is a one-in-200,000 chance, which is why the risk a code carries is relay, not guessing. And a report-only week turns a vague worry ("will this block people?") into a count you can act on.
