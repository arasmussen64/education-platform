<Intro>

A network with more weights than data points can fit the training rows perfectly, noise and all, and the loss curve that proves it is the one every practitioner learns to dread: training loss still falling, validation loss turning upwards. This topic makes that happen on purpose, sixty noisy points and a network of two hidden layers of 64, and then applies the three standard cures, **weight decay**, **dropout** and **early stopping**, measuring each against the plain run, before showing the one cure that always works: more data.

The main resource is **Wikipedia's _Overfitting_ page**, which has the picture of a curve fitting noise and the machine-learning section; about 10 minutes. The _Dropout_ page explains the training-time scaling and why nothing changes at test time, and the _Early stopping_ page covers patience and keeping the best weights. Watch for:

- **Generalization**: the gap between the training score and the held-out score.
- **The scaling in dropout**: why survivors are multiplied by 1 / (1 - p).
- **Patience**: why early stopping waits before it stops.

</Intro>

<Recap>

- **Overfitting** is the training loss falling while the validation loss, after a low point, rises: the network is fitting the noise of its training rows, which rows it hasn't seen don't share. In the example the plain run's training loss reached 0.028 while its validation loss fell to 0.478 at epoch 28 and climbed to 2.115 by epoch 300. Training longer makes it worse. The opposite failure, both losses high, is **underfitting**: a model too small or a run too short.
- **Weight decay** adds `decay * W` to every gradient (L2 regularization), a steady pull towards zero that each step applies: under plain gradient descent with no other gradient a weight shrinks by the factor `1 - rate * decay` per step (Adam rescales the pull as it does any gradient, but the direction is the same). Large weights have to earn their keep against the data. At 0.03 the example's validation loss ended at 0.386, holding near its low. It assumes a good model is a smooth one with small weights; it hurts when the data needs sharp boundaries or the network is already too small, and libraries usually leave biases out of it (this topic's `Adam` decays them too, for simplicity).
- **Dropout** zeroes each hidden value with probability `p` during training, a fresh random mask every step, and scales the survivors by `1 / (1 - p)` (**inverted dropout**) so that the expected activation is unchanged and nothing needs correcting at evaluation. No unit can rely on a particular other unit, so the network learns spread-out, redundant features. It is a layer with two modes: `Sequential.train()` turns dropping on, `Sequential.eval()` turns it off, and `fit` and `evaluate` must switch between them. At 0.5 the example's validation loss ended at 0.571: the rise is shrunk, not abolished. It hurts small layers and slows training.
- **Early stopping** watches the validation loss after every epoch, keeps a copy of the weights from the best epoch, and stops once the loss hasn't improved for `patience` epochs, then restores the best weights. The patience skips the ordinary wobble of a curve: on the plain run with a patience of 20, training stopped at epoch 48 and kept the weights of epoch 28, the low point. The accuracy there, 0.79, is below the 0.84 of epoch 300: the loss climbed not because more points were missed but because the points still missed were missed with growing confidence, which the log-loss punishes and a count of right answers can't see. Loss moves smoothly and sees that overconfidence, which is why early stopping usually watches it. It is not cheating: choosing when to stop is what the validation set is for; the test set, touched once at the end, is what must stay unseen.
- **More data** is the regularizer that always works, because structure repeats and noise doesn't: twenty times the training rows kept the validation loss at 0.398 after 300 epochs where the small run's reached 2.115. When more data can't be had, **data augmentation** (shifted, flipped, noised copies of what there is) is the next best thing, and the checkpoint's digits are drawn with random shifts and noise, the same idea.
- **What each assumes.** Weight decay: smooth is better. Dropout: redundancy is better. Early stopping: the validation low is the place to be. More data: the new rows come from the same distribution as the old. Each helps when its assumption holds and hurts when it doesn't, which is why the validation curve, not the training curve, is what decides.
- **This topic's `fit`** is The training loop's with three changes: it trains full-batch (sixty rows is one batch, so `batch_size` and `seed` are gone), it switches the network between train and eval modes, and it takes a `patience`; its history still starts after the first epoch.
- **In machine learning** (names you'll meet later): `weight_decay` is the optimizer argument (and **AdamW** applies it to the weights directly rather than through the gradient); `nn.Dropout`, `model.train()` and `model.eval()` are the two modes, and forgetting the switch is the classic evaluation bug; `EarlyStopping` callbacks and checkpoints that save the best weights are this topic's `fit`; and **batch normalization** regularizes a little as a side effect of the noise in its batch statistics.

</Recap>
