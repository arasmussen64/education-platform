The company split its shares 2-for-1. Every share became two, each worth half as much, so an owner of 10 shares at $200 woke up with 20 shares at $100: the same $2,000. The raw prices show a cliff that cost nobody anything, which is why past prices are adjusted before anyone measures a return.

</Puzzle>

*How a trade happens* followed one order. This topic is about what all those trades leave behind: the price data you see on a chart or download as a table, and the traps in it. You'll read price bars, adjust a history for splits and dividends so the returns from *Measuring returns* come out right, and build two kinds of index from three companies. Every price here is made up: real price histories and index levels belong to the firms that sell them and can't be shipped with a course. As everywhere in this module, it's about how things work, not advice on what to buy.

**Bars: open, high, low, close, volume.** Thousands of trades a day are summed up as one **price bar**, or **OHLC**: the **open** is the day's first trade, the **high** and **low** the highest and lowest trades, and the **close** the last. Next to them sits the **trading volume**, how many shares changed hands. A week's bar is built from its days: the first open, the highest high, the lowest low, the last close, and the volumes added up. Returns are measured from one close to the next, as in *Measuring returns*, so a price that jumps overnight between a close and the next open counts. Volume says how much to trust a move: a rise on twice the usual volume is one many people took part in; one on a few hundred shares may be a single eager buyer.

<Story title="The flash crash, 6 May 2010" source="https://www.sec.gov/news/studies/2010/marketevents-report.pdf" sourceTitle="Findings Regarding the Market Events of May 6, 2010" license="US government work (report of the staffs of the CFTC and SEC); linked, not copied">

On the afternoon of 6 May 2010, US share prices, already down over 4% on the day, fell another 5 to 6% in minutes and bounced back almost as fast. Many of the almost 8,000 stocks and funds traded that day fell 5, 10 or even 15%, then recovered.

Some did far worse. Between 2:40 and 3:00 p.m., over 20,000 trades in more than 300 securities happened at prices 60% or more away from where they'd been at 2:40, some at a penny a share, some at $100,000. Buyers had vanished from those order books, and market orders to sell met the only bids left: placeholder quotes that market makers keep far from the real price, called stub quotes.

After the close, the exchanges and FINRA cancelled all of those trades. The lesson for anyone reading price data: a high and a low are single trades. One strange trade can set them, and a day's extremes deserve a second look before you believe them.

</Story>

**Splits.** In a **stock split** a company turns each share into several: in a 2-for-1 split every share becomes two, each worth half. Owners hold the same value in more pieces, but the raw price list shows a 50% fall on the split day. Measured from raw prices, the return over the split is nonsense. The fix is to restate the past in today's shares: divide every close before the split by the split ratio (2 for 2-for-1, 3 for 3-for-1), and leave the closes from the split day on as they are. The result is an **adjusted price** history, and returns measured on it are right. The latest prices stay real, the ones you could trade at today; the old ones change.

**Dividends.** A dividend is cash a company pays its owners. On the **ex-dividend date**, the first day a buyer no longer gets the next dividend, the share is worth about that much less, and its price usually opens lower by about the dividend. Holders lost nothing (they get the cash), so that drop shouldn't count as a loss either. The simple adjustment this module uses: work out the factor `1 - dividend / close`, where `close` is the last close before the ex-date, and multiply every earlier close by it. A $1 dividend on a $50 share gives 0.98, so every earlier close comes down 2%. Each earlier day's return stays the same, and the return across the ex-date now includes the dividend, as if it had been put back into the stock that day. Other ways of adjusting exist. Price tables often show both columns, the raw close and the adjusted close, and returns should always come from the adjusted one.

**Indexes.** An index is one number that follows a group of shares. How it adds them up matters as much as which shares it holds. A **price-weighted index** adds up the share prices (divided by a number, the divisor, that's adjusted whenever a company splits or changes, so the level doesn't jump): a $200 share counts ten times as much as a $20 one, whatever the companies are worth. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, the oldest famous US index, is built this way. A **cap-weighted index** adds up market values, each company's share price times its number of shares, and sets the total to a starting level, often 100: each day's level is 100 times that day's total divided by the starting total. Each company counts in proportion to what it's worth; the S&P 500 is weighted by market value. Its return is the companies' returns weighted by their share of the total value. The same three companies can give two very different returns, because the two indexes listen to different things.

<Recap>

- **A bar** is open (first trade), high, low, close (last trade) and volume. A longer bar takes the first open, highest high, lowest low and last close. Returns go close to close. A high or low is a single trade: check a strange one.
- **Splits:** in an N-for-1 split each share becomes N, each worth 1/N. Divide every close before the split by N; leave the rest. Raw prices show a fall nobody suffered.
- **Dividends:** on the ex-dividend date the price drops by about the dividend. Multiply every earlier close by `1 - dividend / close`, using the last close before the ex-date. Other methods exist.
- **Adjusted prices** restate the past in today's terms; the latest prices stay real. Measure returns on adjusted prices, never raw ones.
- **Price-weighted index:** the sum of prices (over a divisor); high-priced shares count most. **Cap-weighted index:** the sum of market values (price × shares), set to 100 at the start; each company counts by what it's worth. The same companies give different returns.
- How things work, not advice: every price here is made up.

</Recap>
