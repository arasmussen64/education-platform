<Intro>

"Hypervisors" showed that a cloud VM is a slice of a physical machine, the host, shared with other customers' VMs. This topic says what the slice is made of and how to read it from the inside: how many real cores you are renting, why big machines have near and far memory, how burstable sizes ration their CPU, how to tell whether the host or your own settings are slowing you down, and how to choose a size from a week of numbers. Several questions ask you to work out a number; use a calculator whenever you like, because the skill is setting up the sum.

**Sockets, cores and threads.** A server's board has one or two **sockets**, each holding a processor chip. A chip holds many **cores**: complete processors, each running its own stream of instructions. A core can also keep two streams going at once, feeding the second into whatever parts of the core the first leaves idle, as it does while the first waits for memory: that is **simultaneous multithreading (SMT)**, which Intel calls **Hyper-Threading**, and each stream is a **hardware thread**. Linux counts every hardware thread as a CPU of its own (a **logical CPU**) and schedules work on each. Picture a building (the host) with floors (sockets), flats on each floor (cores), and in each flat one kitchen and two desks (threads). Two people at the two desks get more done than one, but they share the kitchen, so not twice as much: Google's own page says SMT "can increase application throughput significantly" for general and disk- or network-heavy work, and "can hinder" it when both threads of a core are busy computing. Each core also has small, very fast memories of its own, the **L1 and L2 caches**, holding what it used last; the bigger **L3**, or **last-level cache**, is shared by many cores of a chip.

`lscpu` describes it all. Multiply three of its lines: `Socket(s)` × `Core(s) per socket` × `Thread(s) per core` = `CPU(s)`. `lscpu -e` lists every CPU with its NUMA node (below), socket and core, and shows which CPU numbers are the two threads of one core, which are called **siblings**: on most x86 servers Linux numbers the first thread of every core, then the second threads in the same order, so on a 96-CPU machine CPU 0's sibling is CPU 48. `lscpu -p` prints the same as lines for scripts. Inside a VM, `lscpu` shows what the hypervisor presents, and its manual warns that this "is typically different from the physical (host) system". AWS says its Nitro hypervisor passes the real layout through; Microsoft tells you to read `Thread(s) per core` in the guest. Your own lab machine is a guest too: you will see whatever Docker Desktop's VM presents.

**What a vCPU is.** A **vCPU** is the unit a provider sells, and an **instance type** (or **size**) is one row in its table: a name like `m7i.large`, its vCPUs and memory, and a price; sizes of the same design make a **family** (`m7i`), and choosing among families is Module 12's subject. On most x86 sizes a vCPU is **one hardware thread**: AWS's table lists `m5.xlarge` as 4 vCPUs on 2 cores, Azure says "Azure allocates 2 threads per core - these are called vCPUs", and Google says "each hardware thread is called a virtual CPU". On Arm sizes, whose chips have one thread per core, and on a few AMD sizes, a vCPU is **a whole core**. All three let you set **threads per core** to 1, which switches SMT off and halves the vCPUs, and none makes it cheaper:

| | AWS | Azure | Google Cloud |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A vCPU on most x86 sizes | one thread; two per core | one thread; two per core | one thread; two per core |
| A vCPU that is a whole core | Graviton (Arm) sizes; AMD's C7a, M7a and R7a | Arm sizes (Cobalt 100, Ampere); AMD's Fasv7 | Arm sizes; AMD's Tau T2D and H4D |
| Threads per core = 1 | "CPU options" | "VM vCore Customization" | "threads per core" |
| What you pay then | the same price | the same price | still billed for every vCPU of the type |

**What a neighbour can take, measured.** "Hypervisors" showed what a host keeps apart (memory, devices) and what it doesn't (steady speed). AWS describes its side of this plainly in the Nitro paper's section "Side-channel protections in the broader EC2 service" (two paragraphs of it are on the reading list). A **fixed-size** instance, any size that isn't burstable (AWS says "fixed performance"), has its cores "pinned" to it: they never run another customer's work. Two customers never share a core at the same moment, though some sizes can share the last-level cache. And burstable instances (below) are **overcommitted**: the host has promised them more CPU, all told, than it has, because most of them are quiet most of the time, so they take turns on its cores. So on a fixed-size instance, `st`, steal (Module 1's CPU topic: time the hypervisor gave your virtual CPU to someone else), should sit near zero. A busy neighbour can still crowd what lies outside your cores, the shared L3 cache, the path to memory, the disks and the network, and none of that shows as `st`: you see the same work taking longer. On **burstable** sizes the host really does hand your core to others, and that is where steal lives. AWS's own troubleshooting article lists three causes of high steal (a busy neighbouring instance, an overloaded hypervisor, and "The CPU credit balance for a burstable instance is depleted") and calls it best practice to keep steal under 5%. Microsoft's and Google's pages read for this topic don't say what a guest sees when it is held back, so don't count on steal there either way.

**Burstable instances are a bucket.** A **burstable instance** (AWS's T family, Azure's B-series, Google's shared-core E2 sizes) costs less because it isn't meant to run flat out. It has a **baseline**, a share of each vCPU it may use all the time, and runs above it by spending **CPU credits**. One credit is one vCPU at 100% for one minute. Think of a bucket with a tap and a drain. The tap runs at a fixed rate, vCPUs × baseline × 60 credits an hour. The drain runs as hard as the machine works: vCPUs × CPU use credits a minute. Below the baseline the bucket fills, up to its limit, about a day's earnings; above it, the **credit balance** falls. A `t3.medium` (2 vCPUs, a 20% baseline: 24 credits an hour, at most 576) running steadily at 25% spends 2 × 0.25 × 60 = 30 an hour and loses 6: from a full bucket it is fine for four days, and then it isn't. A test of an afternoon would never show it. Azure states the same sum per minute: ((base × vCPUs) − (CPU % × vCPUs)) ÷ 100 credits banked, so a `Standard_B2ls_v2` (2 vCPUs, base 30%) at 50% loses 0.4 a minute, and the 60 credits it starts with last 150 minutes. What happens at zero is where the clouds differ most:

| | AWS T family | Azure B-series (Bsv2) | Google Cloud shared-core E2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The bucket | at most a day's earnings; kept for 7 days after a stop (lost at once on the old T2) | "Max Banked Credits", a day's earnings | none you can see: a small "token bucket" of its own, good for about 30, 60 or 120 seconds at 100% (`e2-micro`, `e2-small`, `e2-medium`) |
| At zero | **standard** mode "gradually comes down to baseline"; **unlimited** mode, the default on T3, T3a, T4g and T8i, keeps going and bills the surplus | "throttled back to its base CPU performance" | back to its share: 12.5%, 25% or 50% of each of its 2 vCPUs |
| The surprise | a slowdown days later (standard), or a bill (unlimited) | a slowdown days later | a slowdown within a minute or two |

Unlimited mode bills the CPU used above the baseline at a flat rate: for T3 on Linux, $0.05 a vCPU-hour, the same in every region (AWS's pricing page, read 2026-11-11). AWS works out that a T3 flat out all the time costs about 1.5 times a fixed-size M5 of the same size: past some average, a fixed-size instance is cheaper. On Azure, use the current Bsv2 sizes: Microsoft has announced the retirement of the first B-series sizes (`B1s`, `B2s`, `B2ms` and the rest) for November 2028. Prices in this track are **on-demand** prices, the hourly list price with no commitment, for one **region** (a provider's group of datacentres in one area, such as US East, N. Virginia, each with prices of its own), with a dated table in every question, and **a month is 730 hours** (365 days × 24 hours ÷ 12).

**Stolen, capped or simply busy.** "The VM is slow" has several different stories, each with its own witness and its own fix. Module 1 taught most of the witnesses; this table puts them side by side, in the order to ask the questions: is CPU being stolen? Is something capped? Is something simply full? The witnesses that are new here (steal counted over an interval, pressure, the `memory.high` throttle and a pin) are explained after the table.

| What you see | Whose doing | The witness | What helps | What doesn't |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `st` high; the VM does less than it asks for | the host's | steal over an interval in `/proc/stat`; `%steal` on every CPU in `mpstat`; on a burstable size, a credit balance at 0 | a fixed-size instance or unlimited mode; for a busy host, a dedicated host | `nice`, cgroup settings, restarts |
| `st` 0, CPUs partly idle, a service slow in bursts | the machine's own CPU quota | `nr_throttled` rising in the service's `cpu.stat` | raise the quota, or remove it | a bigger VM |
| `st` 0, a service alive but crawling, swap in use | the machine's own memory throttle, or too little memory | `high` rising in the service's `memory.events`; `memory.current` pinned at `memory.high`; memory pressure; `vmstat`'s `si` and `so` | a throttle above what it really uses, keeping its `memory.max` wall; or more memory | more CPUs |
| `st` 0, one service queuing on CPU 0 while other CPUs idle | the machine's own CPU pin | `taskset -cp PID`; `AllowedCPUs` in `systemctl show` | a CPU list that fits, and a restart | a bigger VM |
| `st` 0, one CPU at 100% `%usr` in `mpstat`, the rest idle, no pin | the program's: it uses one thread | the busiest CPU's `%usr` | fixing the program to use more threads | more vCPUs |
| `st` 0, `r` above the CPU count, `id` near 0, no limits | nobody's: the machine is too small | `vmstat`'s `r` against `nproc`; CPU pressure | more vCPUs | changing limits that aren't set |

A disk working flat out at its allowance is one more cap of the machine's own; the next topic, "Memory tiers and disks", measures it.

*How steal is counted.* The first line of `/proc/stat` is `cpu` and ten counters of time since the machine started, in **ticks** (hundredths of a second on most machines): user, nice, system, idle, iowait, irq, softirq, steal, guest and guest_nice. The first eight add up to all the time there was (guest time is already counted inside user), and steal is the eighth. One reading is history since boot and means little. Take two, a minute apart, and divide the growth of steal by the growth of all eight. `mpstat -P ALL 5 1` does the same sum for each CPU over five seconds, with `%steal` in its ninth column.

*Pressure.* Three files, `/proc/pressure/cpu`, `memory` and `io`, each answer one question: for what share of the last 10, 60 and 300 seconds was work waiting for this? The `some` line counts time when at least one task was waiting, the `full` line time when every task was. Each service's control group has the same three files (`cpu.pressure`, `memory.pressure`, `io.pressure`), so the question can be asked of one service.

*A throttle, not a wall.* Module 1's `memory.max` is a wall: reach it and the kernel kills. `memory.high` is a throttle: go over it and the kernel slows the group's processes and takes memory back from them, to swap if there is any, counting each time as `high` in `memory.events`, and never kills. In a unit it is `MemoryHigh=`. A throttle set below what a service really uses is how a service ends up alive, `active`, and a hundred times slower. A restart sets these counters back to 0, so write down what you saw.

*A pin.* `taskset -c 0 COMMAND` runs a program on CPU 0 only, and `taskset -cp PID` shows which CPUs a process may use. For a service, systemd's `AllowedCPUs=` does the same through the service's control group. Two things to know. An empty `AllowedCPUs=` takes the setting away but leaves a running process on the CPUs it had until it restarts, so check the process, not only the setting. And `systemctl set-property NAME SETTING=VALUE` changes a running service at once and saves the setting under `/etc` (in `/etc/systemd/system.control`), while `--runtime` keeps it under `/run`, which the next boot empties: a fix that must last goes in `/etc`, by `set-property` without `--runtime` or in a drop-in.

**NUMA.** On a big server each socket has its own memory attached. The CPUs of a socket reach their own memory quickly and the other socket's more slowly, across a link between the two: **non-uniform memory access, NUMA**. Linux groups each set of CPUs with its memory into a **node**. Picture two towns, each with its own workers and its own warehouse: a worker fetching from the other town's warehouse makes a longer trip every time. `numactl --hardware` prints each node's CPUs, its memory and what is free, and a table of **distances**: 10 means local, 21 roughly twice as far. `lscpu` has the same nodes in its `NUMA node0 CPU(s)` lines, both threads of each core on the same node. A database whose CPUs are on one node and whose memory landed on the other is slower for no visible reason; `numactl --cpunodebind=0 --membind=0 COMMAND` keeps both on node 0 (and, its manual warns, fails an allocation when node 0 is full rather than using the other). It matters on big sizes, for databases and for high-performance computing: Azure's HBv3 VMs see 4 nodes. Most small VMs say `NUMA node(s): 1`, and then there is nothing far to avoid.

**Right-sizing from a week of numbers.** To choose a VM's size you need its busy hours, not its average. A **percentile** answers that: sort the samples from smallest to largest, and the 95th percentile is the value that 95% of them are at or below. Taking the sample at position 95% of the count, rounded up, is the **nearest-rank** way to find it: a week of 5-minute samples is 7 × 24 × 12 = 2,016 of them, and the 95th percentile is the 1,916th, so only the busiest hundred are above it. The average undersizes, because the busy hours, when people use the service most, sit well above it; the largest sample oversizes, because one runaway job then decides what you pay for all month. **Headroom** is room left free on purpose, for growth and short bursts, and a sizing policy states it. Say a VM has 32 vCPUs, and at its 95th percentile uses 22% of them, 7.04 vCPUs, and the policy says the busy hours may fill at most 60% of the new machine: it needs 7.04 ÷ 0.6 = 11.7 vCPUs, so the 16-vCPU size. Memory is sized on its **peak**, because running out of memory isn't a slowdown that passes, it is an out-of-memory kill: a peak of 40 GiB with at most 80% in use needs 40 ÷ 0.8 = 50 GiB, so 64. Then take the cheapest size that meets both. A burstable size fits only when the average per vCPU is at or under its baseline and the peaks are short. AWS's Compute Optimizer, the second reading, works the same way: it sizes on a percentile you choose (P90, P95 or P99.5) with a headroom you choose.

<Recap>

- **Sockets × cores per socket × threads per core = CPUs** in `lscpu`. A hardware thread (SMT, Hyper-Threading) is a CPU to Linux; two threads share a core's parts and do more than one but well short of two. Siblings are numbered apart: CPU 0 and CPU 48 on a 96-CPU server.
- **A vCPU** is one hardware thread on most x86 sizes (two per core) and a whole core on Arm and a few AMD sizes. Divide by threads per core to get cores. Switching SMT off halves the vCPUs and never lowers the price.
- **Neighbours:** a fixed-size instance's cores are its own, so `st` should sit near zero; a neighbour can still crowd the shared cache, memory, disks and network, which never shows as `st`. Burstable sizes take turns on cores, and spent credits show as steal on AWS.
- **A burstable instance is a bucket:** it earns vCPUs × baseline × 60 credits an hour, spends vCPUs × CPU use a minute, holds about a day's earnings. Steady load above the baseline empties it, however long that takes. At zero: AWS standard comes down to the baseline, AWS unlimited (T3's default) bills the surplus, Azure throttles to base, Google's shared-core sizes burst for seconds only.
- **Stolen, capped or busy:** steal (the eighth counter of `/proc/stat`, over an interval) is the host's doing, and nothing inside the VM fixes it. `nr_throttled` in `cpu.stat`, `high` in `memory.events` and a CPU pin are caps of the machine's own. `r` above the CPU count with no steal and no limits is a machine that is too small. Each has its own fix.
- **`memory.high` is a throttle, `memory.max` a wall.** Pressure files say what work is waiting for. `AllowedCPUs=` pins a service; an emptied one needs a restart; `set-property --runtime` is gone at the next boot.
- **NUMA:** a node is CPUs with their own memory; far memory (distance 21) is slower than local (10). Keep a process's CPUs and memory on one node (`numactl --cpunodebind --membind`) on big machines; a small VM has one node.
- **Right-size:** CPU on the 95th percentile (the 1,916th of 2,016 sorted samples), memory on the peak, each against the policy's headroom; then the cheapest size that fits. A burstable size only if the average per vCPU is at or under its baseline and the peaks are short.

</Recap>
