<Intro>

TCP and UDP showed one limit on how fast a connection can send: the receiver's window. This topic is the other one, the sender's guess at what the network between them can carry, and the algorithms that make that guess. The common ones slow down at every lost packet, so on a long path that loses a few packets at random they run at a fraction of the link; a newer one, BBR, measures the path instead. Then **QUIC**, the transport under **HTTP/3**: what it changes, how a browser finds out a site speaks it, and what happens when a firewall blocks it. The labs use the shop's server, `shop`, which sends the nightly backups across a long private link to the DR site, `dr1`, and serves the shop's site to the office.

**Two windows.** **Congestion** is what happens at a link or a router that has more packets arriving than it can send on: its queue fills, and when the queue is full, packets are dropped. A sender can't see the network, so it keeps a number of its own, the **congestion window** (`cwnd`): how much data it dares to have in flight, unacknowledged, at once. It may have in flight at most the smaller of the congestion window and the receiver's window. Picture lorries: the receive window is the size of the warehouse at the far end, the congestion window is how many lorries the sender dares put on the motorway at once. A sender that doesn't know the motorway starts with a few lorries and adds more until some get stuck. `ss -tni` shows the congestion window as `cwnd:21`, in segments of `mss` bytes, beside `rtt:51.089`, the round trip in milliseconds, and `send 4761573bps`, which is just cwnd × mss × 8 ÷ rtt: one window per round trip, as a rate.

**Slow start.** A new connection begins with an **initial window** of 10 segments (RFC 6928: at most 14,600 bytes), and for every segment acknowledged it adds one more, so the window doubles every round trip: 10, 20, 40, 80. This is **slow start**, though it's anything but slow; it ends at the first loss, or when the window reaches the **slow-start threshold** (`ssthresh`), the sender's note of where trouble began last time. The consequence is that a small transfer is counted in round trips, not in bandwidth. In its first four round trips a connection can deliver at most (10 + 20 + 40 + 80) × 1,448 = 217,200 bytes, so a 200 KB page needs four round trips whatever the link's speed: 400 ms to a server 100 ms away, on a 10 Gbit/s link or a 10 Mbit/s one. That's why distance matters even on a fast link.

**Congestion avoidance: Reno and CUBIC.** After slow start, the window grows slowly and is cut when a packet is lost. **Reno**, the classic algorithm, adds one segment a round trip and halves the window at a loss: **AIMD**, additive increase and multiplicative decrease, which draws a sawtooth. A loss shows up in one of two ways. Usually the receiver keeps acknowledging the last byte before the gap, and three of those **duplicate acknowledgements** make the sender resend the missing segment at once (**fast retransmit**) and cut its window. Only when the acknowledgements stop altogether does the sender wait out its timeout (TCP and UDP's backoff) and start again from a window of one segment. **CUBIC**, the default in Linux, Windows and Apple's systems (RFC 9438), cuts the window to 0.7 of what it was, then grows it back along a cubic curve: fast at first, flattening as it nears the window where the last loss happened, then probing past it. Its growth depends on the time since the loss, not on the round trip.

Both are **loss-based**: they read every lost packet as a sign of congestion. On a clean wired network that's true. On Wi-Fi, a mobile network or a long path, packets also get lost at random, and a loss-based sender cuts its window for each one anyway. The **loss rate** is the share of packets lost, written p: 1% is p = 0.01.

**The ceiling, in numbers.** For Reno's sawtooth, the average window comes out at about 1.2 ÷ √p segments (RFC 3649), and the sender sends one window per round trip, so one connection carries at most about

> **1.2 × MSS ÷ (RTT × √p)**

On the lab's link (1,448-byte segments, a 50 ms round trip, 1% loss) that's 1.2 × 1,448 × 8 ÷ (0.05 × 0.1) = about 2.8 Mbit/s, on a 50 Mbit/s link. CUBIC does somewhat better than the formula (the lab measured about 5), but it has the same shape, and the shape is what to remember: **twice the round trip, half the speed; four times the loss, half the speed** (the square root of 4 is 2). Two ways round it: several connections at once, each with its own window (copy tools and download managers open eight or more), or a sender that doesn't treat random loss as congestion.

**Bufferbloat.** The opposite problem, on a clean path. A loss-based sender keeps growing its window until something drops, so when a router has a deep queue, the sender fills it, and every packet then waits in it. In the lab, CUBIC on the clean link reached its 46 Mbit/s with `rtt:213` against `minrtt:50`: 160 ms of the round trip was queue. That's **bufferbloat**: a full link with a full queue, and the delay that comes with it, for every other connection sharing the link too.

**BBR.** **BBR** (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time), developed by engineers at Google, keeps a model of the path instead of reacting to loss. The comment at the top of Linux's own `tcp_bbr.c` gives the model: the **bottleneck bandwidth**, the highest delivery rate it has seen over the last ten round trips, and the **minimum RTT**, the shortest round trip over the last ten seconds, which is the path's own delay without queues. It sends at the bottleneck rate by **pacing**, spacing its packets out evenly instead of sending bursts, and keeps about one bandwidth-delay product in flight, enough to fill the path and no more, so it builds little queue. It moves through four states: STARTUP (doubling, to find the rate), DRAIN (emptying the queue it made doing so), PROBE_BW (most of its life, gently testing for more) and PROBE_RTT (a short dip, to measure the round trip again). In the comment's words, "The core algorithm does not react directly to packet losses or delays". In the lab, through the same 1% loss, BBR got about 45 Mbit/s against CUBIC's 5, with `bbr:(bw:48746488bps,mrtt:50,...)` in `ss`: its model of the path, 48.7 Mbit/s and 50 ms.

It has costs. It resends every packet that was lost but doesn't slow down for them, so it **retransmits** more: about 150 packets in 6 seconds in the lab, against CUBIC's 30. Sharing a link with CUBIC flows, it can take more than a fair share (Geoff Huston's tests, on APNIC's blog, 2017), and behind a **traffic policer**, a box that drops whatever exceeds a set rate, its probing causes steady loss. Versions matter: the BBR in mainline Linux is the first version (the kernel's comment names none, and a March 2025 post on the BBR developers' list says mainline still has 1.x; nothing found since says otherwise). **BBRv3**, which reacts once loss passes 2% in a round trip, is specified in the IETF's draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-07 (2 November 2026, intended as Experimental).

**Who chooses, and how it's set.** Congestion control runs where data is **sent from**. A server that receives uploads gains nothing from BBR for those uploads: the clients send them, with the clients' algorithm. For a server's downloads, its own setting counts (a connection it accepts takes the algorithm its listening socket had). Three places set it:

- for the whole machine, the `sysctl` setting **`net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control`**, for new connections. `sysctl -w` sets it until the next boot; a line in a file under `/etc/sysctl.d/` (Routing taught them) sets it at every boot. The kernel loads an algorithm such as `bbr` the first time something asks for it, and `net.ipv4.tcp_available_congestion_control` lists the ones loaded;
- for one connection, a program can choose with the socket option **`TCP_CONGESTION`** (a program not running as root may choose only from `tcp_allowed_congestion_control`);
- for one test, **`iperf3 -C bbr`**. iperf3, which TCP and UDP used, measures one connection's speed: `-t` sets the seconds, and its last two lines count what the sender handed its kernel (`sender`) and what arrived (`receiver`), which is the one that matters.

Many guides also set `net.core.default_qdisc = fq`, a queueing discipline that paces packets. A container's or a lab machine's network namespace has no such setting (only the machine's first namespace does: `sysctl` says `cannot stat /proc/sys/net/core/default_qdisc`), and it isn't needed: without fq, the kernel's comment says, "TCP stack falls back to an internal pacing". The lab's fixes leave it out.

**Where the three clouds stand on congestion control:** a virtual machine sends with whatever its own kernel is set to, so on AWS, Azure and Google Cloud alike it's the image's choice and then yours: CUBIC, the default in Linux, Windows and Apple's systems (RFC 9438), unless the image or you change it. What the clouds' own load balancers and CDNs use towards users is theirs to choose, and the pages read for this topic don't say.

**QUIC: why.** HTTP/2 sends a page's many requests and answers at once over one TCP connection, each as its own **stream**: **multiplexing** (HTTP and TLS teaches HTTP/2). But TCP is one numbered stream of bytes, the postcards of TCP and UDP. If six images ride one connection and card 40 is lost, cards 41 to 60 wait in the pile, even the ones that belong to other images, because TCP hands on nothing past a gap until it's filled. That's **head-of-line blocking**: one lost packet stalls every stream. The other problem is **ossification**: boxes on the path that read and rewrite TCP's headers (the firewall in TCP and UDP that stripped the window scale option is one) let through only what they recognise, so TCP can hardly change any more, and it lives in every operating system's kernel, which changes slowly.

**QUIC: what.** **QUIC** (RFC 9000) is a new transport that does TCP's job and more, built on UDP so that boxes on the path let it through, and run by the application rather than the kernel, so it can change with an app update. What it changes:

- **One handshake instead of two.** HTTPS is HTTP inside **TLS**, the protocol that encrypts it (HTTP and TLS teaches it). Over TCP, the TCP handshake takes a round trip, and TLS 1.3 one more, before the first request can go. QUIC has TLS 1.3 built into its own handshake: one round trip, and the request goes. In the lab, over a 100 ms path, `curl -w` showed TCP connected at 0.100 s, TLS done at 0.203 s and the first byte of the answer at 0.304 s; over QUIC, the answer arrived at 205 ms. A client returning to a server it knows can even send its request in its very first packets, **0-RTT**, with a catch HTTP and TLS explains: such a request could be replayed by someone on the path.
- **Streams that don't block each other.** QUIC numbers each stream's data on its own, so a lost packet stalls only the stream it belonged to.
- **Connection IDs.** TCP knows a connection by its two addresses and ports; when a phone moves from Wi-Fi to 5G and its address changes, every TCP connection breaks. A QUIC connection is known by **connection IDs** in its packets, so the server still recognises it: **connection migration**. In this version of QUIC only clients migrate, and a server may refuse it (the lab's Caddy says `disable_active_migration=1`).
- **Almost everything is encrypted,** packet numbers and acknowledgements included, so boxes on the path can't read or meddle with QUIC's own headers. Only the IP and UDP headers stay readable, because routers and NATs need them. `tcpdump` shows nothing but `UDP, length 1200`.

Three numbers come with it. The client pads its first datagram to at least **1,200 bytes**, which proves the path carries datagrams that big (QUIC refuses to run over a path that can't, Layers and packets' MTU), and until the client has proved its address is real, the server may send it at most **three times** what it received, so a forged address can't turn a server into an amplifier. And QUIC has its own idle timer, `max_idle_timeout` in each side's handshake (30 seconds from the lab's Caddy), the smaller of the two sides' values winning. Because QUIC rides on UDP, a NAT's UDP timer (often 30 seconds, TCP and UDP showed) can forget a quiet QUIC connection first; QUIC's standard says sending something every 30 seconds keeps most boxes from losing their state, and its PING frames do that. A client's own log shows the rest: the lab's `gtlsclient` prints every packet after decrypting it, with the milliseconds since the start.

**HTTP/3 in practice.** **HTTP/3** is HTTP carried over QUIC, always on UDP. A browser can't know in advance that a site speaks it, so it first comes over TCP, and the answer says so with the **Alt-Svc** header: `alt-svc: h3=":443"; ma=2592000` means "HTTP/3 is on UDP port 443 of this same site; remember that for 2,592,000 seconds" (30 days; without `ma`, 24 hours, RFC 7838). The browser tries HTTP/3 next time. It can also learn it before connecting at all, from the site's **HTTPS DNS record** (a DNS record, as IPv6 put it, is one fact about a name that DNS hands out; this kind lists how to connect), which can say `alpn=h3` (RFC 9460).

If something on the path drops UDP 443, the HTTP/3 attempt gets no answer, and the browser goes on over HTTP/2 without a word: a **fallback** nobody sees. In the lab, with UDP 443 dropped on shop, `h3get` gave up after 5 seconds while `curl` over TCP answered "hello over HTTP/2.0". The site works; it's only slower on bad links, which is a fault nobody reports. So turning HTTP/3 on is two changes: the server listens on UDP 443 and advertises it, and **every firewall on the path lets in `udp dport 443`**. In the labs the server is **Caddy**, a web server that speaks HTTP/3 by default (Ubuntu's nginx can't yet: nginx has HTTP/3 only since 1.25.0). Its settings are one file, the Caddyfile, whose global options at the top list the versions it speaks, `protocols h1 h2 h3`; `systemctl reload caddy@shop` makes it read the file again, and keeps the old settings if the new file has a mistake.

When HTTP/3 helps: users far away or on mobile networks, links that lose packets, pages with many requests, phones that change networks. When it doesn't: clean links inside a data centre, one big download (a single stream has no other streams to unblock), and networks that block UDP, as many companies' do outside DNS. It also costs servers more processor time than TCP with TLS, which have had decades to mature and get help from the hardware.

**Where the three clouds differ on HTTP/3** (each read 2026-11-12):

| | AWS | Azure | Google Cloud |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| At the edge | CloudFront offers HTTP/3 to viewers (they need TLS 1.3), with connection migration; Route 53's HTTPS records can announce it | Front Door's FAQ lists HTTP, HTTPS and HTTP/2, not HTTP/3 | The global external Application Load Balancer advertises HTTP/3 by default (its setting `NONE`, "Automatic"; `DISABLE` turns it off); the regional one doesn't offer it |
| Load balancers | The Network Load Balancer passes QUIC through to targets untouched (since 2025-11-13), keeping a connection on one target by its connection ID | Application Gateway's HTTP/3 is a **preview**, for the front end only | (the same load balancer) |
| Towards backends | not stated on the page read | Application Gateway speaks HTTP/1.1 to backends | HTTP/3 is only between clients and the load balancer |

A load balancer that ends HTTP/3 itself speaks an older HTTP to the servers behind it: the round trips QUIC saves are the ones between the user and the edge, where the distance is.

<Recap>

- **Two windows:** in flight at most min(congestion window, receive window). `ss -tni` shows `cwnd` (segments of `mss` bytes), `rtt` and `send` = cwnd × mss × 8 ÷ rtt.
- **Slow start:** 10 segments, doubling every round trip until a loss or `ssthresh`; a short transfer is counted in round trips (four for 200 KB), whatever the link's speed.
- **Loss-based (Reno, CUBIC):** every loss is read as congestion. Reno halves, CUBIC cuts to 0.7 and regrows on a cubic curve; three duplicate acknowledgements mean fast retransmit, a timeout means a window of 1. Ceiling about **1.2 × MSS ÷ (RTT × √p)**: twice the RTT, half the speed; four times the loss, half the speed. Parallel connections multiply it. **Bufferbloat:** on a clean path they fill deep queues (`rtt` far above `minrtt`).
- **BBR:** models the bottleneck bandwidth (highest delivery rate, 10 round trips) and the minimum RTT (10 seconds), paces at that rate with about one BDP in flight, and doesn't react directly to loss. Costs: more retransmissions, an unfair share against CUBIC, steady loss behind policers. Mainline Linux has version 1; BBRv3 is the IETF draft.
- **The sender chooses:** `net.ipv4.tcp_congestion_control = bbr` in `/etc/sysctl.d/` on the machine that **sends**, per socket with `TCP_CONGESTION`, per test with `iperf3 -C`. Read iperf3's `receiver` line. fq isn't needed; BBR paces by itself. In any cloud a VM sends with its own kernel's setting: CUBIC, unless the image or you change it.
- **QUIC:** over UDP, in the application; TCP's and TLS 1.3's handshakes in one (the first byte a round trip sooner); streams without head-of-line blocking; connection IDs, so a client can change address (migration); almost everything encrypted against ossification. First datagram padded to 1,200 bytes; a server sends at most 3 times what it received before the client's address is proved; its own `max_idle_timeout`, and PINGs to keep NATs' 30-second UDP state. 0-RTT only on returning, and replayable.
- **HTTP/3:** found through `alt-svc: h3=":443"; ma=...` (24 hours without `ma`) or an HTTPS DNS record with `alpn=h3`; with UDP 443 blocked, browsers fall back to HTTP/2 silently. Turning it on: the server listens on UDP 443 and advertises it (Caddy: `protocols h1 h2 h3`), and every firewall allows `udp dport 443`. Helps far, lossy, mobile, many-request users; not clean data-centre links or one big download.
- **The clouds:** CloudFront offers HTTP/3 to viewers, with migration; AWS's NLB passes QUIC through, sticky by connection ID; Google's global external Application Load Balancer advertises it by default, between clients and the load balancer only; Azure Application Gateway's is a preview, backends on HTTP/1.1; Front Door's FAQ lists HTTP/2, not HTTP/3.

</Recap>
