<Intro>

Module 4 handed you two losses and said "use these". This topic says where they came from. A model with a parameter gives every possible dataset a probability; the **likelihood** is that probability for the data you actually saw, read as a function of the parameter; and **maximum likelihood** picks the parameter that made your data most probable. Taking logs turns the product into a sum, and minus that sum is a loss: squared error when the noise is normal, cross-entropy when the labels are coin flips. The losses were never arbitrary.

The main resource is **StatQuest's _Maximum Likelihood, clearly explained_**, seven minutes that slide a normal curve across some data until it fits best; then the _Principles_ and _Examples_ sections of Wikipedia's article for the same idea with the formulas, about 20 minutes. Watch for:

- **The data is fixed and the parameter moves:** a likelihood is not the probability of the parameter.
- **The log turns products into sums** and leaves the maximizer where it was.
- **The normal's best mean is the sample mean**, and its best spread divides by `n`.

</Intro>

<Recap>

- **Likelihood.** For a model with parameter `θ`, `L(θ)` is the probability (or density) of the data you observed, with the data fixed and `θ` varying. Six heads in eight flips: `L(p) = p**6 * (1 - p)**2` (times a constant that doesn't depend on `p`). On a grid of 99 values of `p` it peaks at 0.75, where it is only 0.01112: the data was unlikely under every `p`, and 0.75 is the least unlikely.
- **Maximum likelihood** picks the `θ` with the largest `L`. Three ways to find it: a **grid** (`np.linspace` and `argmax`), **calculus** (set the derivative of the log-likelihood to zero: `6/p - 2/(1 - p) = 0` gives `p = 6/8`), or **descent** on the negative log-likelihood with Module 4's loop (200 steps from 0.5 reach 0.75).
- **Why the log.** `log L(θ)` has the same maximizer, because the log is increasing; it turns a product of many small probabilities into a sum of logs, which doesn't **underflow** (get too small for a float and become exactly 0) and differentiates term by term. The **negative log-likelihood** (NLL) is a loss: smaller is better, and it is what every training loop minimizes whether it says so or not.
- **A coin:** the **maximum-likelihood estimate** (MLE) of the bias is `heads / flips`. **A normal:** the MLE of the mean is the sample mean (`x.mean()`, 169.9 for the worked example's thousand heights), and the MLE of the spread divides the squared deviations by `n`, which is NumPy's default `x.std()` (9.89); the textbook's `n - 1` version is a different, slightly larger estimate, not the maximum-likelihood one.
- **Least squares is maximum likelihood** when each target is the prediction plus normal noise with the same spread: the log-density of one observation is a constant minus `(y - ŷ)² / (2σ²)` (`ŷ` is the model's prediction for that `y`), so the log-likelihood of all of them is a constant minus the sum of squared errors over `2σ²`. Maximizing it is minimizing the sum of squared errors. The normal assumption is the fine print.
- **Cross-entropy is maximum likelihood** when each label is a Bernoulli with the model's `p`: the probability of the labels is the product of `p` for the ones and `1 - p` for the zeros (0.3024 for the worked example's four labels), and its negative log, 1.196, is exactly the cross-entropy sum `-Σ [y log p + (1 - y) log(1 - p)]` (`Σ` means "add up over every example"; Module 4 averaged this sum over the examples, and both have the same minimizer). Minimizing cross-entropy maximizes the likelihood of the labels.
- **What each loss assumes:** squared error, normal noise of one spread everywhere; cross-entropy, independent coin-flip labels. When the data breaks the assumption (a few wild **outliers**, values far from all the others, say), the loss is the wrong one, and a different likelihood gives a different loss.
- **In machine learning:** "minimize the loss" and "maximize the likelihood" are the same sentence; `model.fit` in any library is this topic with the gradient supplied by backpropagation; and Bayes' rule from the last topic is the same likelihood multiplied by a prior, which the next modules use when the prior matters.

</Recap>
